The key points from Chancellor John Healey's first Budget

Article text

The Chancellor gave with one hand and took with another in the Budget, with savers and buy-to-let landlords paying the price. John Healey delivered a cautious first Budget, announcing cost-of-living help but warning of the 'global economic storm' ahead. With borrowing costs at a 30-year high and fuel and energy bills rising, Healey pledged to provide 'breathing space' for households and businesses. That included energy support for households, but the £100 payment will only go to those living in houses in council tax bands A to D. It was trailed as a 'do no harm' package of limited tax hikes helping to pay for cost-of-living support through a raise in bank taxes, while delaying big spending decisions on defence, benefits and social care. Yet, savers will strongly disagree with the 'do no harm' claims, as the Chancellor staged yet another raid on their interest - adding a tax rise to another tax rise from Rachel Reeves that hadn't come in yet. Tax rates on savings interest will shoot up from their current 20 per cent, 40 per cent and 45 per cent, to 24 per cent, 44 per cent and 49 per cent. We also heard from the Office for Budget Responsibility, which published its forecasts and laid bare the economic realities facing the Chancellor. He said: 'First, by giving Britain some breathing space on the cost of living and the cost of business, 'Second, backing the enterprise of British business to unleash innovation and growth in every postcode 'And third, by tackling the big challenges on energy, on national security, on public service transformation and on housing.' Borrowing is set to fall to 4.2 per cent this year, reducing to 1.8 per cent in 2030-31, while UK debt as a percentage of GDP is up to 83.2 per cent this year and 83.8 per cent next year, before dipping to 81.7 per cent in 2031-32, the Chancellor said. The OBR forecasts the Government will spend £14billion more on debt interest this year as Britain battles 'global economic storms'. Meanwhile, GDP is expected to grow 1.2 per cent this year before falling back to 1 per cent next year, and rising to 1.6 per cent in 2028. Energy bills Healey has already announced he will axe standing charges on gas bills, in a move that will benefit millions of households - but experts warn those in larger homes could be worse off. The Treasury said Healey will today ask the energy regulator Ofgem to remove standing charges 'in 2027 or as soon as possible after'. He said he will set out similar reforms for electricity bills. 'From this point, our priority is first and foremost cheaper energy, with clean power key to achieving this,' he said in his statement. He will extend the VAT cut on domestic energy bills through the winter and pledged to take 25 per cent off business energy bills from April. And finally, households in council tax bands A to D will receive a £100 energy payment paid directly into their accounts. > What will changes to standing charges mean for your energy bill? Low-income pensioners avoid income tax The Chancellor has confirmed pensioners whose sole income is the state pension will not pay income tax. Those relying on just the state pension had been set to pay income tax on it for the first time from next April, as the annual amount would breach the income tax threshold of £12,570. Fuel duty The 5p fuel duty cut will be extended to July 2027, the Chancellor confirmed. Duty paid on motor fuels has been frozen since 2011, except for the temporary cut which was introduced in 2022 when oil prices spiked after Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine. Savers and landlords face higher tax rates From April 2027, savers and landlords who are basic rate taxpayers will pay 24 per cent income tax on interest and rents, up from 20 per cent today. It follows the two percentage point hike imposed by former Chancellor Rachel Reeves in last year's Budget. > Savers and landlords hammered with income tax raid in Budget as Healey ratchets up Reeves' hike Business rates relief John Healey said he will make it so that 70,000 more small businesses do not have to pay any business rates. He will increase the threshold for small business rates relief for retail, hospitality and leisure businesses from £12,000 to £18,000. He said this would be welcome news for firms such as bakeries and hair salons, which 'make the places we live and work, the places we love.' For larger retail and hospitality firms, he announced a freeze on rates in real terms, 'so that none face an above-inflation rise for two years.' Vape shops and adult gaming centres will be exempt. And Healey confirmed all business rates will be retained locally by the end of the decade, with 20 per cent of revenue going to mayors from next year. Gambling tax hikes Healey said he would fund tax breaks for the High Street by hiking gambling taxes, including increasing machine games duty from 20 per cent to 25 per cent for higher stakes machines. But he said he would protect pubs and seaside arcades from the increases. Minimum wage rise The national minimum wage and national living wage will increase by 3.9 per cent for 2.3 million workers, Healey confirmed. Cash Isa cut reversed Healey has abandoned his predecessor Rachel Reeves' plans to cut the cash Isa allowance from £20,000 to £12,000, and confirmed he will work with the sector to create a new 'starter Isa' to reward first-time investors. Tax on banks John Healey has confirmed a temporary rise in the bank surcharge, currently levied at 3 per cent on top of the 25 per cent corporation tax rate, by an extra 3 percentage points to 6 per cent to help fund energy support. 'Banks will pay an extra 3 per cent on the Bank Surcharge, this will be time limited, to pay for immediate support on energy bills. Starting in January, ending 15 months later,' he said. 'I’ve made my choice at this Budget: asking a bit more of our banks to support ordinary people, raising taxes on gambling to support good growth on every high street.' > Bank shares jump after Budget tax raid 'not as bad as feared' Help for entrepreneurs Healey has pledged more support for entrepreneurs with a ‘founders’ relief’, a 10 per cent rate on lifetime gains up to £10million for the most successful founders. The Chancellor has introduced a higher trading allowance, meaning people can earn £3,000 without paying any tax, from £1,000 previously. He will also launch a new young entrepreneurs allowance, which will cover a year of living costs for founders. Defence Healey, who resigned as defence secretary under Sir Keir Starmer, said he would fill a £4.7billion gap in the Defence Investment Plan. 'In today's budget, I am fully funding the Defence Investment Plan, including through capital reprioritisation across Whitehall.' 'This honours the commitment made both by me - and by the prime minister'. He said he is 'getting work under way' to reach spending of 3 per cent of GDP on defence by 2030 and will set out how he will hit that target at next year’s spending review. Britain will also commit £10 billion of additional economic and military support to Ukraine, and half a billion in urgent military funding this winter. Your First Home scheme The Government will launch a new first time buyer Isa to replace the lifetime Isa and confirmed plans for its 'Your First Home' scheme, which will allow buyers to get on the property ladder with a 2.5 per cent deposit.
