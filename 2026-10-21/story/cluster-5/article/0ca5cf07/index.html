Prince Harry says he 'wants it to work out' with his family... but takes a jab at those who 'don't want to'

Article text

Prince Harry has today insisted he wants to reconcile with his family, but has been forced to deny he was taking a 'swipe' at Prince William after declaring: 'Some people don't want it to work out.' The Duke of Sussex, who has not spoken to his brother for four years, admitted in an interview with GQ that he is grieving his 'complicated relationships' with the King and the Prince of Wales. Asked directly whether he really wanted to heal the rift with his brother and father, Harry replied: 'Of course I do. With everything else that's going on in the world, the most important thing is to reconcile. That's the key point.' But in remarks widely interpreted as a dig at William, he added: 'Some people don't want it to work out. I want it to work out.' Hours after the interview was published a Sussex spokesman insisted that the remark was not aimed at his older brother as speculation mounted over who Harry was referring to. He said: 'It was absolutely not intended as a swipe at his brother or any other member of his family. 'The comment was made in a general context and was not directed at his family in any way.' Harry's reconciliation plea came as the King and Queen, as well as William and Catherine, gathered in Windsor today for the State Visit of the Sultan of Oman. The duke also offered an insight into why he and Meghan have moved back to the UK, revealing that his 'attitude has changed' after the 'battles' they have endured since they quit as working royals and moved to the US six years ago. Harry said 'scars stick around forever', but urged people to change their mindset over past grievances and try to let them go, adding that his own family rift was 'relatable' and 'continues to come up with me the whole time with people that I speak to'. Prince Harry says he 'wants it to work out' with his family now he is back in the UK The Prince and Princess of Wales during the State Visit of the Sultan Of Oman in Windsor today The King, pictured with the Queen and Kate, has not seen the Sussexes since their return in August The duke, who is on speaking terms with the King but has not seen him since moving back to Britain, admitted that returning to the UK had brought his painful family rift back to the fore. Asked if he thinks he could repair 'this wound', the Duke of Sussex replied: 'Yeah of course, I think every wound… look, scars stick around forever but… what your perception is and what your mindset is to these things that have happened, certainly letting go of the past, that's something that I would encourage everybody to do. 'If there's certain things from the past that's still affecting your everyday, that's harder to let go of. But yeah, for me, there's enough drama in the world and enough drama in people's lives than to continue with issues with family.' The interviewer interjected 'it's not worth it?' and Harry said: 'That's hard to say to people when you're in it.' Harry was the latest guest on GQ's One More Question interview series. It was recorded in late September and released today. Harry was asked whether he was grieving his fractured relationships with his father and brother, he said: 'Yes, of course. I think from a family perspective, again, another massively relatable issue for so many people, sibling disagreements, broken relationships between kids and their parents, kids and one parent. 'That continues to come up with me the whole time with people that I speak to, so I know how relatable it is. People maybe look at us and go, "How are you gonna work it out? Maybe that will help me work my stuff out".' He added: 'As a 42-year-old man with two kids, a boy and a girl... you start to look back at your past and your parents as to what you have carried from them, what they've handed over to you, what you want to take on and what you don't.' Harry said he believes there are elements of the past that his family would 'all like to leave behind us' so they could 'work for the greater good'. In the wide-ranging interview, which also focused on mental health and his grief over the loss of his mother, Harry also insisted he did not want preferential treatment simply because he is a royal. He added: 'I certainly don't want any sympathy. That's where we're way past that. Why? Because I'm a prince. I've got a support network around me, I've done different forms of therapy, and I put in the work. I wonder where I would be if I didn't. It could be a very different situation.' The Sussexes stepped down as working royals in 2020 for personal and financial reasons. Soon afterwards, the couple gave their bombshell interview with Oprah Winfrey, in which they accused Harry's family of racism and of being uncaring when Meghan said she had felt suicidal. William was forced to respond publicly to the racism allegations, declaring: 'We're very much not a racist family.' Relations deteriorated further following accusations Harry publicly levelled at his brother in two tell-alls: his memoir Spare and his Netflix documentary Harry & Meghan. In his autobiography, released months after Queen Elizabeth II died, Harry described his 'archnemesis' brother 'Willy' allegedly physically attacking him and knocking him to the floor during a row over Meghan. Harry and Meghan also claimed in their Netflix series that Kensington Palace had lied to protect William when it issued a statement denying a report that he had bullied the Sussexes out of the Royal Family. The duke was speaking about grief, a subject on which he has also recently released an online MasterClass, which may have earned him up to $100,000. Harry claimed that he and Meghan were ready to return to Britain after fighting 'battles' in the country over the past six years. Their 'attitude has changed' and it was now 'slightly easier' to come back, he said. 'I think once you've been through it, once you've been able to talk about it, and raise awareness to it, then people see it for what it is, it makes the environment slightly easier. It's not going to necessarily stop the behaviour, but I think the behaviour might, my hope that it will, change,' Harry said. He added: 'What happened back then was then. What's happened over the last six years has been a continuation of that. Some battles that had been fought, and necessarily so. 'With everything else that's going on in the world, there's aspects of the past that I'm sure all of us, me, my family, and some of the British Press, that we'd all like to leave behind us and work for the greater good.'
