The catastrophic floods that struck Nepal and Tibet are reported to have surged as high as 650ft, killing at least 538 people and leaving more than 1,500 missing. Desperate families of those who were visiting the mountainous region have now flown out in a bid to find their loved ones. Among them is the wife of 54-year-old Pravin Atmakur, who was leading a group of 77 pilgrims to Mount Kailash in Tibet when the floods struck. Manjula Atmakur arrived in Kathmandu on Friday morning after travelling from Britain to search for her husband, who has not been heard from since the disaster. The couple both work as consultant psychiatrists for the NHS, while their son studies at Trinity College, Cambridge. Mrs Atmakur told the Telegraph in Nepal: 'I am emotionally broken. I don't know how my husband is. In which situation. I want him back.' She added: 'I came here with the only agenda that I want to take Pravin back home alive.' She believes he had completed the pilgrimage and was returning from Tibet into Nepal when the floods struck at around 9am on Wednesday. Nepal is now facing what officials described as a scene of 'devastation beyond imagination' after a glacier collapse unleashed a 'tsunami from the sky', sending torrents of water crashing through villages and tourist hotspots. Footage showed the mudslide racing down the Trishuli River valley and through Gyirong Port, a major border crossing between Nepal and Tibet used by thousands of pilgrims travelling to Mount Kailash each year. Mrs Atmakur learnt of the disaster from her brother-in-law and immediately began preparing to travel to Nepal. Her 19-year-old son Nalin, who studies engineering at Trinity College, Cambridge, was travelling at the time. She waited for him to return so they could fly out together. She reached Kathmandu on Friday and went directly to the British embassy, but said she had received little help. Mrs Atmakur said her husband had devoted much of his life to helping others and that she now wanted authorities to help bring him home. A millionaire family-of-four from Surrey including a former Mrs India UK are also among Britons missing in Nepal. Ex-banker and beauty pageant winner Surabhi Sahu, 44, her businessman husband Santosh, 46, their son Spandan, 18, and daughter Syna, 13, had been on an adventure trek across the Nepal-China border. Mr and Mrs Sahu ran a successful technology company in London together before he set up a thriving pharmaceutical business. The family live in a £1.3million six-bedroom house in Cobham, Surrey. There are hopes the tourists are alive and waiting to be rescued, but phone lines are down and the military has struggled to reach the mountainous area. Mrs Sahu is a chemistry graduate from Mumbai who became a banker before moving to Ireland to work for Dell Computers. Her husband Santosh is an Irish citizen, and the family moved to Surrey where they became beacons of their community. In 2018, Mrs Sahu won the Mrs India UK beauty contest – after being encouraged to enter it by her family – which she described as 'a dream come true'. She has posted photos of herself visiting favourite spots of England, including the Cotswolds. Her former banker husband Santosh has gone from strength to strength and is the founder and chief executive of Charac, a digital platform helping pharmacies improve deliveries to patients. Their son Spandan is due to start a law degree at Durham University. A former grammar school pupil and her NHS worker mother are also among those missing after travelling to the region on a pilgrimage. Nepalese authorities have declared them missing, and their worried family said they have received no contact from the UK Foreign Office. Nelly, an 'ambitious' former pupil of the Invicta Grammar School for girls, in Maidstone, Kent, hopes to go to university next year, her cousin Nickie Chhetri told the Daily Mail. She has just completed a sixth-form course in health and social care at Uxbridge College, in London. Her relatives were last in contact with her when she was staying at the Kailash Hotel in Timure on the Nepal-China border, where she was waiting for Chinese customs officers to approve her visa. Ms Bector's husband, Bhupesh Bector, told The Times he was frantically checking his iPhone to check her location but it had not changed for more than seven hours. He flew out last night, saying: 'I know that she's in Nepal for sure, so even if they find her today or tomorrow, I need to be there.' Foreign Office minister Stephen Doughty has said the number of 33 Britons missing is still the same, but 'may fluctuate'. Speaking to Sky News, Mr Doughty said: 'This is a devastating and deeply worrying time for the families, and obviously our teams here in this centre and in Kathmandu and Beijing are in close contact with families, and that team is going out to the region to support families that may choose to travel out. 'But the number that's been reported of 33 is certainly the information that we've had from the Nepal government, that's public, but obviously these figures may fluctuate. 'This is a very, very complex situation, very remote areas, and you'll have seen the scale of the devastation. 'We're in close contact as well with the tour operators. There are a number of tours operating, those on religious pilgrimages, those trekking in the region. 'But we know this is going to be a deeply devastating and difficult time, and the job of our teams will be to try and work with those families to get them the information they need in their hour of need.' The flooding was likely caused by a huge chunk of a glacier that broke off and temporarily dammed a river, according to preliminary investigations by scientists. That may have led to the backed-up water surging downstream with enormous force a few hours later, they suggested. Scientists are still piecing together information from satellite and ground-level data to figure out exactly what happened to cause the flood. The US Geological Survey on Wednesday reported a magnitude 5.2 glacier collapse that sent debris flowing downstream with catastrophic impact on communities. Rescuers continue to search for the thousands still missing, as Nepali and Chinese authorities warned of further flooding from a lake that formed upstream after Wednesday's disaster and was already overflowing. They urged people in potentially affected areas to move to safer ground. On Friday, footage emerged showing locals sprinting for their lives as a catastrophic flood tore through a town in Nepal's Rasuwa region on Wednesday. The deadly deluge is one of the country's worst natural disasters in recent memory. In the six-minute long clip, terrified people scramble to escape on foot and in vehicles as powerful floodwaters tear through the area, dragging debris along with them. Meanwhile, panic was sparked and fears of a new tsunami mounted when a lake dammed by avalanche debris in the Nepalese region burst through its banks. Chinese authorities have since deemed the risk as manageable, allowing halted rescue operations to resume. Helicopter rescue operations were abruptly halted on Friday morning with locals in Rasuwa valley seen running to higher ground after water started flowing form the newly-formed body of water. The lake had formed up stream in Tibet some six miles from Gyirong port, the border crossing with Nepal that was engulfed by mud and floodwater on Wednesday. China's Ministry of Water Resources said on Thursday that the lake held around two million cubic meters (70 million cubic feet) of water and was expected to receive another three million cubic meters (106 million cubic feet) over the next three days.
