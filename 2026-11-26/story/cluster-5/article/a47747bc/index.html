Prince William gets on with business as usual as two men appear in court accused of scaling drainpipe to break into future king's Norfolk family home

Article text

Prince William gets on with business as usual as two men appear in court accused of scaling drainpipe to break into future king's Norfolk family home It was business as usual for Prince William as he undertook a string of public engagements as two alleged burglars appeared in court accused of breaking into his Norfolk home. The heir to the throne visited an ambulance garage before holding a reception for paramedics at Windsor Castle. Both events were long-planned and went ahead despite the troubling incident at Anmer Hall, the country retreat on the Sandringham estate used by the Prince and Princess of Wales and their family, on Monday night. At the South Central Ambulance Service, the future king spoke to local crews and heard the story of a paramedic who said that she felt able to stay in her job after receiving trauma therapy. Hannah Flower, 37, told Prince William that she had eight virtual sessions of EMDR, eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing, which helped her cope when she suffered from burnout at work and the stress of her younger daughter's leukaemia diagnosis. Now aged five and in remission, Flower's daughter Edith was diagnosed with cancer when she was 22 months old. Later, a friend's death proved to be a 'tipping point' for the paramedic and caused her to seek help. Speaking about her therapy, Mrs Flower, a mother-of-two, told the prince: 'I learnt about the service through burnout. I call it witchcraft. It really worked for me. It definitely worked.' William, who worked as a Search and Rescue pilot with the RAF as well as with East Anglia Air Ambulance, nodded and said: 'There's techniques and tips for everyone. We all deal with things in a different way but I'm pleased that's here. There are still gaps across the country.' He knows what we're going through. He has that lived experience.' The prince heard that nine per cent of the ambulance service's 999 call outs related to mental health. He was told that while young people were coming into training there were not necessarily enough jobs to go round once they had qualified. Kirsten Willis-Drewett, head of ops at the south central ambulance service, told the prince: 'There's a good pipeline but we don't necessarily have the roles.' Sitting on sofas in the crew room at Wexham ambulance centre in Slough, the ambulance service that covers his own home of Forest Lodge, William asked: 'There's been a lot of talk about AI. How will AI fit into your lives?' Ms Willis-Drewett told him that there had been trials where it had proved to be 'helpful' for screening for illnesses. The prince then moved upstairs to a board room where he asked staff to 'brainstorm' and said: 'If we're brainstorming, if we think big for the future, what could be a real benefit?' He talked about 'wearable tech' and said: 'We were talking downstairs about the idea of wearable tech where someone could put something on a bracelet, for instance, on a casualty and be able to effectively stream the data back to an expert, or you guys, and then be talked through how to help to give people the confidence to help because people want to help, they just don't know how.' Prince WIlliam appeared engaged in conversation with the attendees, having worked for two years himself as an air ambulance pilot The reception went ahead despite an attempted break-in at Anmer Hall, Prince William's country retreat on the Sandringham estate, on Monday William hosted medical staff and volunteers at Windsor Castle to mark the 25th anniversary of the Royal College of Paramedics, of which the prince is patron He also heard about a pioneering scheme run at the trust which focuses on providing proactive and preventative counselling for paramedics. Holly Wells, project lead for the trauma support programme, explained that it was formed in two parts: a teaching module that helps paramedics to spot psychological trauma in themselves and others; and six one-to-one coaching and counselling sessions to improve resilience. Wells told the prince: 'My hope is we see a reduction in sickness levels as our staff are better supported and their home life isn't affected by psychological trauma.' Before leaving he returned to the crew room to pose for a joint photograph and paused to look at a board showing pictures of staff on nights out and in fancy dress. William said: 'Now, who can I see here?' When he was told that one of the men was pictured on the board, William joked: 'Appropriately dressed, I hope!' Afterwards he hosted medical staff and volunteers at Windsor Castle to mark the 25th anniversary of the Royal College of Paramedics, of which the prince is patron. After greeting guests in the castle's green room, William went through to the crimson room where a Christmas tree had already been decorated. 'Oh God, last but not least,' William said with a laugh when he finally reached Carl Smith, an advanced paramedic who worked alongside the prince for two years. After joking that Smith, 53, brought 'chaos' with him, William added: 'To be fair, he's extremely good. I take the mickey out of him a lot, but he's actually quite good.' Smith, head of critical development for emergency and critical care at the Royal College of Paramedics and an on-call firefighter, last week won the Tiptree breakfast hero award. William said: 'He can't get too big a head, I've told him that. His remit is expanding across the country.' The prince served as an air ambulance helicopter pilot with East Anglia Air Ambulance service from March 2015 to July 2017, flying as part of a crew with Smith for much of that time. Speaking afterwards, Smith said: 'I worked with him the time that he did the air ambulance at Cambridge. Excellent pilot and just part of the team. He would do anything any team member would do. 'He said "last but not least" and we had a bit of a laugh but that's him, isn't it.' He added: 'He has been on some really serious jobs with us. Very traumatic. He probably doesn't talk about them but he has been involved in some serious jobs in his time with the air ambulance. 'And he will talk about that privately but he has seen a lot of nasty stuff and been exposed. The pilots will set up blood for us so they are there in the thick of it. 'If a patient is bleeding to death we will say to the pilots, "can you get the blood ready?" We have to work as a team.' William announced his patronage of the Royal College of Paramedics in January 2025, a year after the college gained its royal charter. Meeting staff, the prince told staff and volunteers 'congratulations on 25 years, here's to another 25 years'. Upon arrival at Windsor Castle, William was met by Tracy Nicholls, chief executive of the Royal College of Paramedics, who asked if he had had a good morning. 'Yes, a very good morning,' he replied. 'Lots of ideas brewing, which is dangerous. But they had some really good thoughts and good ideas that could be done to improve life in the role.' As William focused on his busy schedule, two men appeared at Norwich Magistrates' Court charged with burglary and trespass on a protected site following an attempted break-in at Anmer Hall. Police were called to the home, on the King's Sandringham Estate in Norfolk, shortly before 6pm on Monday following reports of suspicious activity and arrested the two suspects nearby. Prince William, Princess Catherine and their children George, Charlotte and Louis were at their main home in Windsor at the time of the raid. Arilen Ndoci, 32, one of the suspects, was pictured today. He appeared alongside his alleged accomplice, Mirgen Doku, 34. The suspects are both 'Albanian nationals who entered the country illegally', the court heard. A third suspect remains at large. Doku, of South Kensington, west London, and Ndoci, of no fixed address, are accused of stealing the 'Duke of Edinburgh's cufflinks' from the dressing room and a clock which is of 'value unknown'. Prosecutor John Cooper told the court that the defendants are suspected of breaking into the property and entering the dressing room by going up a drain pipe. The ten-bedroom Norfolk mansion was a wedding gift to the Prince and Princess of Wales from the late Queen Elizabeth II. Considered the family's favourite bolthole, the Waleses regularly spend weekends and holidays at Anmer Hall and it was reportedly Kate's 'sanctuary' during her cancer battle. Arilen Ndoci is one of two illegal Albanian immigrants charged with breaking into the Prince and Princess of Wales' Norfolk home Mirgen Doku (left) and Arilen Ndoci appeared at Norwich Magistrates' Court on Tuesday alongside a dock officer The two suspects allegedly tried to break into Anmer Hall on the King's Sandringham Estate It was also at the Norfolk residence that she filmed the deeply personal video announcing the end of her chemotherapy treatment. Anmer Hall was built in 1802 and purchased by Queen Victoria in 1862. It has previously been leased to business owners, civil servants and members of the British Royal Family. William and Kate have been residing there periodically since a £1.5million refurbishment was completed in 2014, though their visits became less frequent after the death of Queen Elizabeth and since making Adelaide Cottage in the grounds of Windsor Castle their principal residence.
