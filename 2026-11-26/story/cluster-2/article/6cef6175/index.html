Gordon Ramsay is the perfect choice for Top Gear – but not for the reason you think

Article text

So, Gordon Ramsay will be leading the new, rebooted – well, re-re-rebooted – Top Gear next year alongside a brand-new line-up of co-presenters.  It does make a kind of sense. After all, marching in to sort out once-great institutions has been Ramsay’s MO since his first Kitchen Nightmare back in 2004. And, in a statement, Ramsay professes to have been a car nut since his childhood “growing up a field away from Silverstone, where I would climb over the fences and sneak in for a glimpse of the racing”. No doubt he’s very excited. But he’ll have a job on his hands here. Top Gear is, let’s not forget, coming back from a hiatus that commenced when former presenter Andrew Flintoff was in a horrific crash during filming. It left him in a shocking state both physically and mentally; the Disney+ documentary Flintoff, which details his recovery and ongoing struggles with PTSD, is a genuinely difficult watch. It was hard enough to bring Top Gear around when it was just dealing with people realising they didn’t really like Chris Evans. Flintoff’s crash is not something that will fade from memories any time soon.  So now Ramsay’s at the wheel. We are at the point of maximum Ramsay, the Gordon supremacy: between the recent doc Rise of the Rockstar Chefs, next year’s just announced reboot of Hell’s Kitchen alongside Claudia Winkleman, and that Instagram post where he showed off his six-pack, it feels like he’s been everywhere lately.  It is, on one level, a pretty transparent attempt to reconnect Top Gear with its Jeremy Clarkson-led, bootcut jean-clad glory days. Since Clarkson, Richard Hammond and James May left in 2015, what was one of the BBC’s biggest shows has been home to a rotating line-up of half-remembered hosts, a Sunday night motoring magazine programme equivalent of the Sugababes. All but one host – the late Sabine Schmitz – has been a bloke. Despite things picking up after the Evans interregnum, it felt very much like its best days were in the past. So what do you do when you want to remind people of a shouty man doing power-slides around an airfield? You book perhaps Britain’s most famously shouty man to do some power-slides instead.  Obviously it’s a bit more complicated than that. Clarkson and Ramsay are very different personalities, but both became massive TV stars in the noughties bear pit. Where Ramsay reduced sous chefs to melted nubs for trying to serve him underwhelming devilled crab, Clarkson mocked the puddingy steering of the new Lexus saloon. Their brashness and bullishness is their brand, and you can’t really say that about – to take one name from the teetering pile of post-Clarkson presenters – Matt LeBlanc. And part of it is the fame. There are shades of Nigella joining Bake Off here: a marquee TV show getting a bona fide star in, one who presumably needs the show less than the show needs them. Ramsay is – and I say this with the greatest of respect to Take Me Out’s Paddy McGuinness – the sort of megawatt name Top Gear hasn’t had before. But there is something else here. The Grand Tour, which Clarkson, May and Hammond made together after Top Gear, has just replaced them with two guys who run a motoring YouTube channel and Instagram train guy Francis Bourgeois. This is the way the wind is blowing. Ramsay has managed to parlay his TV fame into a hugely popular TikTok channel (40.8m followers) and YouTube channel (22m subscribers). He’s also a one-man meme machine, his face floating around the internet and in group chats the world over. Ramsay is pretty much the only presenter who can both get the old Top Gear heads interested again and also make any sense at all to anyone under 30.  Ramsay’s sheer star power and understanding of what people want from him on screen does make sense as a way of trying to wipe the slate clean. Whether viewers are ready to move on is another question.
