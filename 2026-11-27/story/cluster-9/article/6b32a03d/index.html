Bank of England reveals cost of the mortgage shock for 6.5M homeowners

Article text

Millions face a mortgage shock, Bank of England warns, with some bills rocketing £6,000 a year The Bank of England has warned that 6.5million borrowers face a jump in monthly mortgage payments, as their fixed deals run out over the next three years. More than 400,000 borrowers will see monthly payments rocket by at least £500 – adding £6,000 a year to their annual mortgage bills. Homeowners face an average hike to their monthly payments of £100 – costing them £1,200 per year, according to new Bank of England figures. Higher interest rates mean the average hit is twice the £50 it expected earlier this year, when it had predicted slightly fewer homeowners – six million – were in line for an increase. The sharp rise in costs will be individually determined by the size of borrowers' mortgages, the length of their remaining term – and the previous rate they managed to fix at. The Bank of England forecasts that for 3.4million borrowers there will be an increase of less than £100 by the end of 2029. A further 2.7million will see monthly payments go up by between £100 and £500. The hardest hit will be a 430,000-strong cohort expected to suffer an increase of more than £500. The Bank of England led by Andrew Bailey raised interest rates in November and has warned on higher mortgage costs The figures from the Bank’s Financial Stability Report underline the squeeze facing households who are also facing steep rises in energy bills and fuel prices – all as a result of Donald Trump’s Iran war, which has pushed up oil and gas prices. It came as the report also sounded the alarm about the growing dangers posed by the artificial intelligence (AI) revolution – which is fuelling overall economic growth but also creating risks because of the vast sums involved in its financing as well as enabling cyber attacks by ‘malicious actors’. At the same time, the Bank announced proposals to ease capital requirements for lenders in a way which could boost lending capacity by £175billion – though could also boost payouts to shareholders. Ofgem revealed yesterday that the energy price cap will shoot up by 26 per cent in January, adding £443 to the average household's bills. The twice-yearly assessment was published as the Bank’s Monetary Policy Committee looks set to increase interest rates just before Christmas. This follows a Bank of England rate hike to 4 per cent this month – with further rises next year forecast by financial markets. In the report, the Bank noted that households debt burdens are rising as rates go up and cost-of-living shocks grow. This burden as a proportion of household income could climb to the highest level since the financial crisis by the end of 2029 should the war continue. The Bank’s findings suggested lower income families were under greater financial pressure, with some struggling to pay debts. Consumers are also shopping around or cutting spending to cope with the intensifying squeeze. Nearly 30 per cent report being under financial pressure while there are also ‘growing concerns’ about the potential for AI to take workers’ jobs. Meanwhile, higher loan-to-income mortgage lending has increased ‘including among some borrower groups that may be more vulnerable to shocks’. The Bank’s wider findings on AI will raise further alarm about the spread of the technology, at a time when US tech giants are expected to borrow more than $300billion this year to finance its development. It noted recent wild moves in stock markets reflecting ‘repeated swings in investors’ expectations about the future earnings of artificial intelligence companies’. That has added to the dangers posed by volatile energy markets and rising government borrowing costs across the globe. The Bank said the huge expansion in AI financing ‘in a form that is increasingly complex, circular and opaque’ was deepening the interconnectedness of global markets ‘making it increasingly likely for stress in one area to propagate to another’. It also underlined the need for banks to beef up their defences against disruption and cyber attacks – an issue also highlighted by outages of Google Cloud and Microsoft, which showed how problems at a single provider ‘can affect many firms at once’. A key factor is the growing use of so-called ‘open-weight’ AI models, many of them Chinese, which can be sourced for free unlike many of those provided by the big US tech firms. These become ‘increasingly available to malicious actors’ as they can be run more cheaply and with fewer safeguards than other models.
