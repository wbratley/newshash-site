430,000 UK homeowners face £500-plus jump in monthly mortgage payments

Article text

More than 400,000 UK homeowners will face a £500-a-month increase in their mortgage payments over the next three years, as rising interest rates heap extra pressure on households, the Bank of England has said. The Bank’s financial policy committee said on Thursday that out of the 7 million borrowers coming off fixed-rate deals before December 2029, about 6.5 million would have to cough up extra funds to cover their home loan payments. For about 3.4 million borrowers the monthly increase would be less than £100, while for a further 2.7 million it would be £100-£500. The increase would be even greater for those borrowers coming off much lower interest rates, with about 430,000 likely to see their monthly mortgage payments surge by more than £500. Warnings about the increases came after policymakers at the Bank of England hiked interest rates from 3.75% to 4% this month. The Bank also hinted at another increase in interest rates to combat inflationary pressure caused by the Iran war. The financial markets have predicted three quarter-point rate rises by summer 2027. Over the next year, about 1 million households will see their payments rise by almost £200 a month. The news will put further pressure on Andy Burnham’s efforts to offset cost of living pressures, with the chancellor, John Healey, having used his first budget to promise “breathing space” for hard-pressed consumers. The figures came a day after the energy regulator for Great Britain raised the cap on household energy bills by 26%. The Bank’s deputy governor for financial stability, Sarah Breeden, said it was important to keep in mind that households would “face this [mortgage] shock with strong balance sheets”. She said household debt was relatively low, at a level last seen in the early 2000s. “So they start from a position of some resilience. And secondly … while there’s a distributional effect and some households would be hit more than others, in aggregate we think this is absorbable.” The Bank also raised concerns on Thursday about cybersecurity and AI threats after recent outages at Google and Microsoft laid bare businesses’ growing dependence on outside technology, and how “disruption at a single provider” could “affect many firms at once”. The Bank’s governor, Andrew Bailey, told a press conference that policymakers were worried about “open weight” AI – open source models, many of which are being developed in China. “These models can be run more cheaply … Safeguards may be disabled or removed more easily. This could make more advanced AI capabilities more readily available and useful to malicious actors,” he said. The Bank also raised fears for the first time about emerging quantum computing capabilities potentially undermining the encryption technology that protects essential financial transactions. Bailey said quantum computing would have been at the top of everyone’s mind this year, but that the surprise development of frontier AI models had overshadowed those concerns. “It’s slightly moved quantum into the background … as you can see, we are very intentionally saying we can’t forget about quantum computing, it’s still coming down the track,” he said. He emphasised that it was crucial, and possible, for banks to start to build up their defences against quantum risks, even while the technology was in nascent development. “It could have powers that go beyond what we’re seeing with frontier AI and have substantial power to undermine cryptography,” he said. The Bank said the evolving risks reinforced the need for “significant and sustained investment by the financial sector in building resilience”, adding that banks needed to have credible arrangements to ensure they could bounce back quickly from any disruption and maintain “vital financial services”. The financial policy committee acknowledged this would place competing demands on lenders’ tech experts and management teams, but added that a failure to take action would “compromise firms’ operational resilience with potential system-wide implications”. The Bank also announced plans on Thursday to further reduce capital requirements for UK banks, as part of a wider package that it said could unleash an extra £175bn in lending capacity. However, extra funding could end up being used to pay out shareholders and investors.
