‘Half price!’ ‘Only two left!’ How to resist the bargains you just don’t need – this Black Friday and beyond

Article text

The other morning a parcel arrived and I was momentarily mystified by its contents. Peering at what appeared to be a shiny maroon and pink cushion cover, the penny eventually dropped. It was the fancy “Sweat Bag” I had bought on impulse when it popped up on Instagram after a particularly trying day. The blurb had convinced me it was the perfect solution for storing sweaty gym gear, ensuring that everything else in my bag remained pristine. “Whether it’s a 6am class before work or squeezing in lunchtime workouts, the Sweat Bag moves with you, wherever your day takes you.” I liked the idea of being this busy, efficient human, but in reality, I generally head straight home after my workouts and collapse beside the nearest biscuit tin. I had no business spending, ahem, £39 on it. If you’ve ever fallen into a similar retail trance only to experience buyer’s remorse, chances are that you, too, have been ambushed by “consumption noise”. Since the rise in popularity of weight-loss drugs we’ve heard quite the cacophony about “food noise”, with users celebrating the welcome absence of cravings. In a similar vein, consumption noise can best be described as the low-level but relentless triggering of a hunger to buy stuff just for the sake of it. “Consumption noise is everywhere. It’s online, it’s outdoors. If you look at the amount of advertising we constantly receive, when we’re on public transport, on the streets, especially in big cities, it’s a lot,” says Emilie Bellet, a former private equity analyst and the founder of the financial education platform Vestpod. This constant exposure is best summed up, she says, by the phrase “‘visual pollution’, a term invented by Marine Tanguy, the author of The Visual Detox. Then, of course, there’s online pollution as well. You buy from a brand one time and you’re on their mailing list, bombarding you with offers, which creates more temptation.” I like to think that I’m good with money. I’m a thrifty Scot who agonises over whether to buy the posh butter or the Aldi dupe. And yet when it comes to marketing tricks, I’m pretty gullible. When I see a tempting offer online flagged with “only two left in stock”, I can feel my heart-rate speed up. Recently, this drove me to buy the world’s most useless slow cooker – it doesn’t have a timer. “I mean, even if it is true that the stock is about to run out, don’t worry. Chances are good that the item will come back,” says Bellet. “For me, the feeling of just putting an item in the basket makes me happy. I get a little hit of dopamine. And then after two days, I realise actually, I don’t really want it, or I’m busy with something else and decide not to buy.” Bellet goes as far as using a Brick blocking device to remove social media from her phone in the early morning and in the evening. The gadget contains a Near Field Communication chip and works with a companion mobile phone app. You choose which apps and websites to block and tap your phone against the Brick to activate. The device is magnetic, the idea being that you stick it on the fridge so you can’t turn your apps back on until you return to the kitchen to tap it. “It creates more clarity, more space for something other than always looking at people trying to sell you stuff,” says Bellet. Another option is to pay £3.99 a month to go ad-free on, say, Instagram. “It sounds like a lot to pay but it’s a gamechanger,” reports a colleague. “Weirdly, now I’m free from falling down rabbit holes into fashion brands I can’t afford, I am drawn to look at the app a lot less.” But the special-occasion buzz around Black Friday and its manufactured sense of urgency can destabilise the best of intentions. “We’re bombarded with these time-limited discounts; perhaps there’s even a countdown timer or warnings about how many other customers are viewing the same item,” says Aarron Atkinson-Toal, the author of a new book, Influenced: The Science of Why We Buy. “Suddenly not buying something can feel like missing out on an opportunity rather than choosing not to spend our money. It’s easy to feel overwhelmed by all that information, all the different offers, and so we start to respond to these quick prompts to ‘buy it now’ instead of carefully considering what we actually need.” Atkinson-Toal identifies an interesting paradox. “Humans are generally risk-averse. We don’t like missing out on something. So with Black Friday-style offers we are forced to decide which is worse for us as an individual – the pain of missing out on the deal or the pain of buying something that you later realise you don’t need. More often than not, we make the purchase.” This explains why even the most mindful among us can sometimes make unwise impulse buys. When I asked around for examples of crazy splurges, the responses were reassuringly plentiful. “I spent a fortune on an Alexander McQueen tartan mini kilt, inspired by Claudia Winkleman on Traitors,” says Ros. “I left it too late to return, and in truth I look ridiculous in it.” Buying for an imaginary version of yourself is not uncommon. Social media marketing is particularly hazardous. “I bought a floor cushion sofa I saw on Pinterest,” says Jane. “Basically a load of big grey cushions masquerading as an L-shaped sofa. Stupidly expensive. When it arrived the boxes filled up my small living room – I didn’t take measurements. It looked silly and was incredibly uncomfortable.” And then there’s that sense of needing retail therapy. “I’ve had a really stressful time at work lately,” says Dave. “Well, that’s my excuse for buying, in the past month, a Gibson Les Paul guitar, a pair of leather trousers and a mid-century modern sideboard.” Situational spending – temporarily suspending budgetary rules according to where you are – can be especially hazardous. Airports seem to dial up the consumption noise by making us feel as though we’re not spending real currency: overpriced tins of biscuits, truffle butter and luxury brand eyeshadow palettes, as totally hypothetical examples. The marketing language used around small purchases can be particularly seductive. “Brands have started using the concept of ‘little treat culture’ to convince people that buying yourself something nice will make you feel better. And if we don’t have the budget to buy big designer items offered by luxury brands, the smaller options they’re selling can give us a boost,” says Atkinson-Toal. “We’re rewarding ourselves with small purchases to make everyday life feel a little better because we’re being bombarded with all this doomsday news. So perhaps we buy a coffee on the way to work, or a fancy pastry. Maybe a small bottle of perfume or a piece of jewellery. Each purchase might be relatively small. But we’re essentially using shopping as a way of managing how we feel.” His top tip for overcoming consumption noise is to stop shopping on autopilot. “The truth is that 95% of our buying decisions are subconscious. We all rely on mental shortcuts to make expedient choices. We might be guided by our emotions or recall familiar experiences. Or we might look to what other people are doing, like friends, family, celebrities or even strangers online. And marketers understand this incredibly well.” He advises getting savvy with marketing tricks, and to pay close attention to selectively good reviews. “Happy customer testimonials or claims about 99% effectiveness are directing your attention away from negative reviews elsewhere. Pay attention as well to ‘priming’ – language such as ‘artisan’ or ‘handmade’ designed to make you spend more.” At this time of year, as collective spending peaks, another factor comes into play and that is the influence of other people. “Consumption tends to be a substitute for social connection,” says Olaya Moldes Andres, a senior lecturer in marketing and strategy at Cardiff University. “Feeling part of a collective social experience like Black Friday gives us a sense of feeling connected to others, even strangers online. We are all highly influenced by what others do.” Her research analyses advertising content to identify the most common hooks brands use to influence us, which we can be alert to. “There are five key things – first status and the idea that we can create an ideal version of ourselves. Another one is making life effortless. Third a sense of belonging, selling us a product that will help us to strengthen and develop social connections. Then there’s the idea of a product that will make us more capable. And finally, escapism and excitement, the idea that we need to have amazing experiences with intense emotions to take us away from our everyday lives.” It’s not so much a question of what we buy as why we buy, she says. “Brands are not so much selling us products as elaborate narratives about the lives we aspire to have.” Moldes Andres suggests, before getting sucked into a buying frenzy, asking yourself if you are being triggered because you’re feeling low, “hangry” or another negative emotion that you believe shopping will fix. “Ask yourself if it might be possible to improve your mood or feel less lonely or anxious by another means than shopping,” she says. “It’s about trying to understand that consumption is only one available option to obtain the feelings you think your shopping basket will give you. The problem is that we have learned a core mechanism to seek happiness through purchasing.” Instead of spending money, you could choose to do other things to feel satisfaction – for example, going to the gym, walking in nature or spending time with loved ones. “And if you can’t think of anything, it’s maybe time to start thinking about more sustainable alternatives instead of shopping.” Listening to music is another way to change the channel, suggests Moldes Andres. Last year she ran a public engagement session at the Green Man festival where she launched a 21-day “buy less” challenge. “My favourite strategy to step away from consumption noise was to get participants to choose a song whenever they felt the urge to shop. The idea is that a song can lift your mood and it will give you two to three minutes to think about whether or not you really need to buy the item. It’s to stop the impulsivity and give you a dopamine boost.” What if you are maybe a bit shallow and want to buy great new sunglasses to show off to other people? “My research has shown that if you are buying to fulfil extrinsic motivations such as to impress others or receive social gratification and admiration from the things we buy, we tend to get less positive emotions from the experience.” Technology has made shopping way too easy, so one way to arm ourselves against consumption noise is to make impulse buys harder. “A great first step would be to remove all your stored card details from frequently visited sites,” suggests Bellet. “That introduces some friction because sometimes you don’t even have time to think, you’ve already bought it. Another thing I recommend, if you’ve been browsing for ages, is to change the environment. Go out, do something else, meet a friend. Try to stop this cycle of looking at things online.” Bellet also recommends taking inventory of what you already own. “Say you’re tempted to buy a beautiful new jumper. If you know you already own 20 other jumpers, ask yourself what this purchase will give you. OK, you will look fantastic when you’re out at dinner. But then it will just join all the others. It’s also good to ask yourself if you need the new thing. I saw a Black Friday offer on a new electric toothbrush. They were 60% off. I said, ‘Wow!’ But then I reminded myself my old one still works fine. I decided to save the £100 or maybe to allocate it for my Christmas shopping. I also keep a list of items that I want on my phone and remove things I’ve lost interest in.” An awareness of how much consumers are being manipulated is also useful. “Influence is more effective when the individual doesn’t know that they are being influenced,” says Atkinson-Toal. “Make a plan, before you leave home or go online, for what you need and what you are willing to spend. Question the bargain. Don’t focus on how much you are supposedly saving but ask yourself whether you’d still want the product if it wasn’t on offer. Because a 50% discount isn’t much of a bargain if you had no intention of buying it in the first place.” However, he does acknowledge that having said all that, it’s not so easy to entirely avoid the hype. “I’d love to say that writing a book about consumer psychology has made me completely immune to marketing. But I’m still really looking forward to Black Friday.”
