I helped organise the letter from Labour MPs urging Andy Burnham to reject Rosebank. This is why | Catherine Fookes

Article text

This week it was confirmed that millions of households are facing yet another winter of unaffordable energy bills, just as we learn that this summer’s heatwaves were linked to more than 5,000 deaths in the UK. The cost of living and climate crises are two sides of the same coin: our continued dependence on fossil fuels is driving up bills, fuelling deadly extreme weather and causing widespread harm to people in this country. It was one year ago that Storm Claudia’s floods swept through Monmouthshire, devastating hundreds of homes, businesses and livelihoods. I know, as flood season is upon us again, how sick residents feel every time it rains. This is why I, along with my colleagues on Labour’s backbenches, have written to the prime minister urging that his government makes the right decision and rejects the Rosebank oilfield. As backbenchers, we are reflecting the concerns of our constituents who watched in horror as wildfires tore through countryside and communities this summer and who now face a winter of worrying about how they will afford to put the heating on. Voicing these concerns is our job as MPs. We are not “rebels”, nor is this a “civil war” inside the Labour party, as some headlines have tried to make out. It is a reflection of the reality that is playing out in our communities. After last month’s approval of the Jackdaw gasfield, my colleagues and I felt uneasy but understood the reasons for that decision – it is relatively small, short-lived and could displace some polluting imported gas at the margin. However, we are clear that the Rosebank oilfield is a different proposition. It contains mostly oil for export, and it is far, far larger than Jackdaw. Most importantly, Rosebank will do nothing to reduce energy bills for our constituents. The oil belongs to private companies who will ship it away to the highest bidder. Claims on its tax benefits are overinflated, rarely accounting for the significant rebates companies claim for developing and decommissioning projects. And in a depleted basin, it will not turn the tide on jobs, which have been in decline for decades. If Rosebank is approved, champagne corks will pop in the offices of oil companies. But it will not make an iota of difference to our constituents’ energy bills. And it will accelerate the climate crisis, which is already pushing up food bills, causing devastating floods in winter and in summer turning our green and pleasant land into a parched, burnt-out expanse. Rejecting Rosebank is the right thing to do domestically, but it is also critical in the wake of last week’s failed climate talks. Cop31 collapsed because some countries refused to accept the imperative to phase out fossil fuels. It is not enough to just accelerate the adoption of renewables. The science shows that we already have more oil and gas in existing projects globally than can ever be safely burned. This means that countries, such as the UK, that are committed to climate action need to act accordingly. Rosebank signals something important, at home and abroad, about our intent. If we cannot make the right decision on this field, which offers so little, then there will be questions about our commitment to dealing with even thornier climate problems. We know that the majority of people in this country support action to protect people now and in the future from escalating climate change. And as we enter our fifth consecutive winter of painfully high bills, our answer cannot be, as the Tories’ was, more drilling. It didn’t help then, and it won’t help now. As MPs, we can see the harms of a warming planet play out now in our communities. Climate change is affecting workers who keep our public transport systems, hospitals and schools running during extreme weather, not to mention our under-resourced firefighters. But it is future generations who will ultimately experience the worst of what is to come. I was proud to stand on the Green Prosperity Plan in our 2024 manifesto. It was one of the pledges that really resonated with people on the doorstep across Monmouthshire – they could finally see a party willing to take on profiteering oil companies and deliver a brighter future of community-owned energy, ample cheap domestic energy supplies from wind and solar and an end to the fuel poverty that blights the lives of so many. As Labour MPs, we are bound to champion working people against the vested interests of the few. In 2026, that means standing with ordinary people living through the reality of the climate crisis and not the private oil and gas supergiants profiting from it. We urge the government to make the right decision. - Catherine Fookes is Labour MP for Monmouthshire
