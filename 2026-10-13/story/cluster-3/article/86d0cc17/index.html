Yorkshire tiger attack - latest: Police say man mauled to death by tiger at wildlife park had visited alone Yorkshire Wildlife Park, which is near Doncaster, remains closed following Saturday's tragedy, with floral tributes left at the entrance to the park A man who was mauled to death by a tiger at a wildlife park on Saturday is believed to have been visiting alone. Police said today that the visitor was not thought to have been accompanied when he was attacked at Yorkshire Wildlife Park, near Doncaster. The man had entered the animal's enclosure at the popular attraction, which remained closed today. A South Yorkshire Police spokesperson said today: "It is not believed that the man who died at the Yorkshire Wildlife Park was being accompanied by anybody." The force will not be naming the man at the request of his family, it is understood. A health and safety investigation has now been launched, with City of Doncaster Council leading the probe. The council said it is "deeply saddened" by the incident. Dan Swaine, the council’s executive director of place, said: "We are deeply saddened by the incident that occurred this weekend and our thoughts and deepest sympathies are with everyone involved, including the family and loved ones of the person who tragically lost their life, those who witnessed the incident and the staff at Yorkshire Wildlife Park, as well as the emergency services who responded swiftly." Key Events Woman who took last photo of man holds back tears in new update Del Taylor, who was one of the last people to interact with the man at Yorkshire Wildlife Park before his tragic death, has shared a new update on the incident. She was visiting the park with her boyfriend when she was approached by the young man, who asked her: "Can you grab a shot of myself with the tiger?" She has now described him as being "so happy" in the moments before he leapt into the tiger enclosure. In a new update on her TikTok she seemed to be fighting back the tears as she spoke about the speculation online surrounding the man. The Yorkshire Hive update The Yorkshire Hive is a free-admission shopping, dining and entertainment destination located adjacent to the entrance of Yorkshire Wildlife Park. The company has confirmed that the Yorkshire Hive will remain open as usual. "There is always a risk of rogue or unusual behaviour" - ZEC member says Dr Paul Rose, a member of the UK Zoos Expert Committee (ZEC) and lecturer in animal behaviour at the University of Exeter, has addressed zoos safety protocols. He says they do take into account risks linked to unpredictable behaviour from members of the public, reports The Independent. He said: “Public behaviour can be unpredictable as we all know,” he said. “There will be mitigation in place, such as high fencing, stand-off barriers, signage and explanation and a code of conduct for how to behave at the zoo – and for visitors to take responsibility for their actions. “Risk assessments will also be undertaken regularly. However, a risk of unusual human behaviour will always remain. “The majority of visitors behave in a way respectful of the animal and environment at the zoo, but there is always a risk of rogue or unusual behaviour and this will continue to be risk assessed and part of zoo personnel training.” Zoo death 'shocking' as cases of people entering enclosures are 'extremely rare' Dr Campbell Murn, a lecturer at the University of Reading and a former member of the Government's advisory body on zoos, has described Saturday's incident as "extremely rare". He said: "UK zoo standards put public and animal safety and welfare at their core, and they affect almost every decision about how a zoo is designed and run. "Animals such as big cats are in the highest risk category. They must always be behind secure primary barriers, with additional stand-off barriers to keep visitors at a safe distance. "Zoos must also have emergency procedures for incidents involving dangerous animals, and staff are trained and regularly drilled in how to respond in such scenarios. "No barrier can be made completely impossible to get past, but incidents like this are extremely rare in UK zoos. That is part of why they are so shocking when they happen." Tragedy reignites debate over keeping big cats in captivity The tragedy at Yorkshire Wildlife Park has reignited debate on social media over the ethics of keeping big cats and other large animals in captivity. Supporters argue that zoos play an important role in conserving endangered species through breeding programmes and safety measures. Critics, however, point out that zoo enclosures are tiny compared with tigers' and lions' natural habitats, citing studies showing that some captive big cats exhibit signs of stress and display behaviours not seen in their wild. Dr Campbell Murn, programme director for zoology at the University of Reading, said that for many people, visiting a zoo may be their only opportunity to see a tiger or another endangered species in person. "This encounter can be powerful. If it inspires people to care about these animals, it can turn into support for protecting them in the wild," he said. Police urge public not to share 'distressing' videos online As a reminder, South Yorkshire Police has urged the public not to share the "distressing" videos of the incident circulating online. The videos have been widely shared on social media and by foreign news outlets. Doncaster's deputy civic mayor, Councillor Nick Allen, who represents a nearby ward, said earlier today that "the speculation online is obviously upsetting and unneeded". The victim will not be named by police at the request of his family, it is understood. Unclear when wildlife park will reopen It is not yet clear when Yorkshire Wildlife Park will reopen. The park announced in a social media post this morning that it would remain closed for the day. In a previous statement on social media, the park said "many, many people" had asked about Altai, the Amur tiger involved in the incident, and confirmed he was "safe and well" and would not be euthanised. It added that members of park staff were "deeply affected" by the incident and were receiving specialist support. Where do Amur tigers come from? Amur tigers, also known as Siberian tigers, are an endangered subspecies, with an estimated 500 remaining in the wild. They live mainly in the forests of Russia's Far East, with a smaller population in north-east China. Their scientific name is Panthera tigris altaica, meaning "tiger of the Altai Mountains", a mountain range at the heart of Asia, where the borders of Kazakhstan, Russia, China and Mongolia meet. The Siberian tiger is the world's largest living tiger subspecies. Males can weigh up to 660lb (300kg) and measure up to 10ft (3m) in length from nose to tail. Tiger involved in attack at Yorkshire Wildlife Park was born in Norway The tiger involved in the attack, named Altai, was moved to Yorkshire Wildlife Park from Dyreparken in Kristiansand, Norway, in February 2025 as part of the European Endangered Species Programme, Norwegian outlets report. Dyreparken biologist Helene Axelsen said Altai was born at the Norwegian zoo in 2021. Latest pictures from scene as investigation into man's death continues Zoo expert: Safety is key to UK zoo standards, but 'determined' people can breach enclosures Dr Campbell Murn, a lecturer at the University of Reading and former member of the Government's advisory body on zoos, explained that determined people can get inside zoo enclosures. "I view it in the same way a determined thief will get into somebody's house or somebody's car, and despite the best security systems and enclosures, sometimes these things happen," he told BBC Radio 4's Today programme. "One of the fundamental approaches of a modern zoo is to give people a wildlife experience and keep them safe so they can learn and they can enjoy being near the animals, but keeping a safety distance that is relevant but not making it too heavy-handed." Dr Murn added that public safety is an "enormous consideration" for zoo standards in the UK.
