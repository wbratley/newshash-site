William Chapman - latest: Inquest updates after man killed by tiger at Yorkshire Wildlife Park William Chapman, 23, was killed after he jumped into the tiger enclosure at Yorkshire Wildlife Park. An inquest into his death is due to be opened later this morning. An inquest to establish the facts surrounding the death of 23-year-old William Chapman is due to open today. Mr Chapman, who was a former child actor, jumped into the tiger enclosure at Yorkshire WIldlife Park as terrified onlookers urged him to "play dead". The Amur tiger, named Altai, then viciously attacked Mr Chapman and he was later pronounced dead at the scene. The hearing is scheduled to take place at Doncaster Coroner’s Court at 10am today; it is listed for William Harry Bruce Chapman. In a statement released on Tuesday evening, William's family said: “It will be for the coroner to decide what part this played in his death. “We would ask everybody to respect that process.” Follow our live blog below for the latest updates. Key Events The role of a Coroners Officer The Coroners Officers, on behalf of the Coroner, obtain and record details of all unexpected, unnatural and violent deaths, reported from various informants. They liaise with interested parties, organise Post Mortem examinations and inform parties of the results. Prepare and issue relevant documents to allow families to get on with their registration and funeral ceremonies. Where required, the Coroners Officer will open investigations including inquests ensuring appropriate documentation is completed. The Coroners officers also conduct identified lines of enquiries, obtaining reports and statements from witnesses, clinicians and outside agencies. Coroners’ officers prepare files for Court, warn identified witnesses of attendance at Court and running Coronial Court hearings. Ancillary duties include dealing with Pathological samples, carrying out identification procedures liaising with foreign Governmental Departments, Advocates and family representative. The inquest opening is due to take place at Doncaster Coroner's Court. On the Inquest listings on the council website it states the hearing will take place in the Coroners Court, Civic Building. The coroner sitting for the 10am hearing has not yet been publicly confirmed. What happens at an inquest opening? Formal start: It is a brief, procedural public hearing where the coroner formally opens the investigation into the death. Basic details recorded: The coroner formally records the identity of the deceased, the date and place of death, and the medical cause of death (if known at that stage). Adjournment: The hearing is almost immediately adjourned (postponed) to a later date to allow time for full police reports, post-mortem results, and further evidence to be gathered. Attendance: The bereaved family and press are allowed to attend, though it is not necessary for family members to be present. What is an inquest The primary purpose of an inquest is to conduct a public, fact-finding inquiry to establish who has died, when and where they died, and how they came by their death. An inquest is not a criminal or civil trial, and the coroner or jury cannot apportion blame, assign guilt, or determine legal or civil liability. An inquest seeks to answer four or five key questions: - Who the deceased person was - Where the death took place - When the death occurred - How the death came about (the circumstances surrounding the death) - The medical cause of death Tragedy reignites debate over keeping big cats in captivity The tragedy at Yorkshire Wildlife Park has reignited debate on social media over the ethics of keeping big cats and other large animals in captivity. Supporters argue that zoos play an important role in conserving endangered species through breeding programmes and other conservation efforts. Critics, however, point out that zoo enclosures are tiny compared with tigers' and lions' natural habitats, citing studies showing that some captive big cats exhibit signs of stress and display behaviours never seen in the wild. In comments following the tragedy, Dr Campbell Murn, programme director for zoology at the University of Reading, noted that for many people, visiting a zoo may be their only opportunity to see a tiger. "This encounter can be powerful. If it inspires people to care about these animals, it can turn into support for protecting them in the wild," he said. Carole Baskin, the star of Netflix's Tiger King and the CEO of Big Cat Rescue, which campaigns to end the exploitation of big cats, expressed her condolences to William Chapman's family in a statement to The New York Post shortly after the tragedy. Baskin added: "We don't yet know all the circumstances that led to this man's death, and it would be wrong to speculate. What we do know is that a tiger is a powerful wild predator, and no amount of captivity, training, or familiarity can change that." Tiger was probably "just playing" - animal keeper says Animal keeper Reece Oliver told Sky News the 30-stone (200kg) tiger was probably "just playing" when it began pawing the man. "If it really wanted to kill him, it would have probably killed him within 10 seconds," he said. "I don't think it knew what he was going to do, because the only thing that goes into that enclosure normally would be enrichment toys or food. "So, I think he was probably just playing, using his predatory instinct, seeing what it was, what he was, and unfortunately it ended up being a fatal attack." 'If they really want to get in, they're going to find a way' Dr Campbell Murn, a lecturer in Conservation Science at the University of Reading, explained that despite UK zoos having to follow set regulations for enclosure designs, incidents like the one on Saturday cannot be eliminated. The expert, who is also a former member of the government's expert committee for zoos, said: "There’s an enormous amount of effort that goes into the securing of public safety for people who are visiting zoos, in addition to the weight that's given to animal welfare in those settings as well. "So it's certainly something that is a very big and integral part of the zoo standards in this country." Dr Murn added that staff at zoos which house dangerous wild animals are trained in emergency responses, explaining that he does not believe the incident at Yorkshire Wildlife Park was a result of a safety failure. Rather, he believes "it was not possible to get there before the visitor was injured and subsequently died". The expert said sophisticated enclosures that make it more difficult for unauthorised people to enter them "would minimise or reduce the risk, but it's never going to truly eliminate it". He said: "Even the most elaborate enclosure, if they really want to get in, they’re going to find a way".
