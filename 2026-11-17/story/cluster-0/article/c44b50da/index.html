The £1bn plan to fix Rachel Reeves’s worst mistake

Article text

There will be a great deal of debate about the findings of Alan Milburn’s review into youth unemployment, which will be published on Tuesday. The Government will welcome the report, and restate how seriously it takes the issue of a million young people stuck out of work. But Milburn’s first and most stark conclusion is one that ministers are reluctant to acknowledge: Rachel Reeves’s decision to raise the tax on hiring people, by increasing employers’ national insurance (NI), has killed many thousands of jobs. That’s implicit in one of the report’s recommendations which has been briefed in advance: Milburn suggests reducing NI for employers who hire younger workers, by raising the threshold at which the tax kicks in. That tax break would be worth £1bn. It was obvious that Reeves’s tax increase would do huge economic damage. If you raise taxes on doing something – in this case, employing people – then you make it harder to do, and you deter some from doing it entirely.  While it’s welcome that Milburn has now formally recognised that in an official government review, it should never have needed saying. Even after a change of prime ministers, one can search in vain for any public recognition by those at the top table that Reeves made a serious mistake. Nor is this simply an error played out on spreadsheets at the Office for Budget Responsibility or the Treasury. For the million young people – and many older workers – who simply cannot find a job, the impact of a tax decision which makes it harder to hire them is a personal tragedy.  Each person who would work if given the chance, but who cannot do so, represents squandered potential for themselves and all of us. Work enriches people’s own lives – financially and psychologically – and improves the stability, resilience and future prospects of their families and wider communities too.  Yet even now, with near-universal recognition outside Government of how wrong the policy was, it seems we must wait longer for it to be set right. Milburn’s review recommends only a partial reduction of NI, over a period of years. He advises that the threshold at which employers begin to pay it, for workers aged 21 to 24, should first be lifted for retail and hospitality businesses, gradually reducing the cost of employing people over a period of five years. It’s hard to understand why this should be the case. The chancellor of the day chose to increase NI on all jobs in one fell swoop, and yet to reverse the error will take years, and be limited to only one age group and two sectors of the economy. The tax rise was wrong, and harmed the economy, so why on earth should we choose to keep it hanging around, indefinitely so for most workers? It’s worth noting that the rest of Milburn’s plan is something he frames as being likely to take a decade to work. That decade may be acceptable to ministers, though goodness knows why. But for the young people involved (and the taxpayers footing the welfare bill in the meantime) it is far too long. We have a million Neets right now. If, in 10 years, that total has come down, it may perhaps be presented as a success on paper. But for anyone stuck in that category who are aged 16 to 24 during that period, it is a disaster. Imagine losing the whole first six years of your career to worklessness, and the lost opportunities resulting from that delayed start. Milburn is right to say that the youth unemployment problem didn’t begin with Reeves’s tax rise. But he’s also right when he says that the tax rise made it a lot worse – so the very first step should be to reverse it fully. By fully, I mean fast, and for all. Liberate employers from these unnecessary penalties for doing the right thing, and they will be able to liberate many more people from unemployment. Footling around only blunts the benefits of reversing the error while adding yet more complexity into our already complex tax system. Milburn’s review was charged with looking specifically at how to get more young people into work, so it’s fair enough that he as limited his focus to under-25s, but the truth is that Reeves’s jobs tax hurt workers of every age. Jobs that might have been created never came to be. Jobs that might have been saved were instead lost. Wages that might have risen were held back as more money went to the Exchequer. This Government is only new, even if many of its problems are rather older, and yet it is already at risk of developing a reputation for big promises of action to be taken at some point after the next election: regionalisation, the National Care Service, reduced energy costs and more are all allotted to the Prime Minister’s vaunted “10-year plan”.  Young people, stuck out of work through no fault of their own, can’t wait that long.
