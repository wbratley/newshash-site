Landmark youth unemployment report suggests Labour's policies are to blame for jobs crisis

Article text

Alan Milburn's review into youth worklessness directly criticises Labour for making Britain's jobs market even harder for young people during its first two years in power. The former Labour health secretary's report argues that tax increases, above-inflation hikes to the youth minimum wage and the new Employment Rights Act have all compounded an already dire situation for under-25s seeking work. Mr Milburn calls on ministers to make it "cheaper, simpler and less risky to hire a young person." His proposals include partially unwinding Rachel Reeves' £25billion employers' National Insurance raid, slowing the pace at which the youth minimum wage is being brought into line with the adult rate. One Whitehall source told MailOnline the report will warn that the raid on employers' National Insurance had a "profoundly negative" impact on the hiring outlook. It also recommends suspending parts of Angela Rayner's flagship employment legislation, particularly provisions abolishing zero-hours contracts. The review says Labour should introduce a legal obligation on ministers to assess how proposed tax changes and regulations would affect young workers' prospects before enacting them. On top of this, the report will warn Labour's bid to equalise the minimum wage paid to young people with the main rate has damaged job prospects, citing evidence that wage costs for young workers have jumped by 65 per cent in five years. Earlier this year, the number of 16- to 24-year-olds classified as Neets, not in education, employment or training, exceeded one million for the first time in over a decade. Six out of ten have never been employed. Mr Milburn's modelling warns that without intervention, this figure will climb to 1.27 million by 2031, pushing the Neet rate from 13 per cent to 16.3 per cent, surpassing the 1.25 million peak recorded in 2011 after the financial crisis. Only Romania had a worse rate of youth worklessness in Europe last year. At the heart of the review is a proposed "young people's guarantee", a legally backed entitlement for all under-25s to a training place or job, coupled with immediate access to mental health support. However, participation would not be optional. Those who refuse the opportunities offered would lose sickness and disability benefits, a measure that has already provoked fierce opposition on Labour's left flank. Mr Milburn wants the National Insurance earnings threshold for young workers raised from £5,000 to £10,000, effectively reversing the former Chancellor's decision to slash it from £9,100. Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch accused Labour of making it "virtually impossible" to recruit young workers, urging ministers to "stop doing the stupid things" that fuelled the Neets crisis. Shadow work and pensions secretary Helen Whately said: "Milburn is right to criticise the tax and regulation burdens Labour have imposed on businesses. But his review is too cautious. "It fails to bring down the welfare bill... and won't make the slightest difference for young people stuck on welfare now." Reform UK's Suella Braverman called the findings "a glaring admission that Labour's jobs tax has failed," warning that a ten-year recovery timeline would "consign another generation to the scrap heap." Meanwhile, Green Party Deputy Leader Mothin Ali called the report's findings "extremely concerning", adding, "Andy Burnham promised an end to 40 years of neoliberalism, but this is more of the same tired proposals. "The Green Party’s true alternative is proper work and training for proper pay, so young people can reach their full potential without the threat of poverty and homelessness." The review's financial projections suggest the reforms would require £5.8billion in annual taxpayer funding by 2036, covering hiring subsidies alongside health, education and skills improvements. Against this, Mr Milburn forecasts £3.6billion in welfare savings and £2.4 billion in additional tax revenues and reduced benefit payments, yielding a net saving of just £200million per year after a full decade. That would leave taxpayers with a net saving of just £200m a year after a decade. The worklessness tsar has claimed that savings will continue to grow beyond the middle of the next decade. Work and Pensions Secretary Pat McFadden is expected to welcome the proposals but will not commit to implementing them wholesale. On Monday evening, he told Labour MPs: "We don't just owe people an income; we owe them a future, and right now many don't get that. "That's why we need a reset of the system."
