‘Complete system reset’: Burnham urged to find ‘political will’ for Milburn’s welfare overhaul

Article text

Andy Burnham will need to “summon the resources and political will” to deliver the youth unemployment tsar Alan Milburn’s proposals to cut benefits and boost investment in employment, industry figures have said.&nbsp; Milburn is set to publish a lengthy plan featuring calls for extensive welfare reform &#8211; including employment subsidies &#8211; in a bid to bring&nbsp;nearly 1m young people currently out of employment, education or training (Neets) into the national workforce.&nbsp; In a speech on Tuesday morning, the former Blair-era health secretary will argue that “half measures will not do” and that a “complete system reset is needed”, posing a political test for Labour parliamentarians who are disgruntled by the prospect of cuts to benefits. Milburn is set to argue against &#8220;crude cuts&#8221; with a warning that savings in the welfare bill could take years to materialise. “A lost generation is not a fact of life, it is a choice, and I believe it is the wrong one,” Milburn is expected to say.&nbsp; “Not just because it is a fiscal crisis or an economic crisis, but because it is a moral one.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; He will add: “It is time for a new settlement that makes work not welfare the future for the next generation.&#8221;&nbsp;&nbsp; Burnham&#8217;s policies &#8216;must flow&#8217; from Milburn&#8217;s review Ahead of his report’s launch, industry chiefs, who offered input into the review taking place over the last 12 months, described proposals as “ambitious” as they urged Burnham to adopt proposals even as a growing number of Labour MPs have expressed concerns.&nbsp; Andy Haldane, the former Bank of England chief economist who advised Burnham during his Makerfield by-election campaign, said there was “no more pressing challenge” in the country than reducing the number of Neets.&nbsp; “Milburn’s report provides the blueprint for doing so. It is now for the government to summon the resources and political will to deliver on that blueprint,” Haldane said.&nbsp; Tina McKenzie, the chair of the industry body representing small businesses, said helping employers with business costs and increasing young entrepreneurship would be “vital for social mobility”. “Bringing forward support for young people who have been economically inactive for too long and giving them a genuine chance to gain experience and build confidence, is an important step and small businesses are well placed as long as public policy decisions now flow from the review,” she said.&nbsp; Some of the Labour backbenchers to criticise Milburn’s proposals include Bell Ribeiro-Addy and Clive Lewis, who publicly backed Burnham’s return to parliament during Sir Keir Starmer’s premiership.&nbsp; Proposals in Milburn’s review include a full reform of the benefits system, increasing the number of apprenticeships, expanding access to mental health support and subsidies for employers taking on risks by hiring young people.&nbsp;&nbsp; &#8216;Decade&#8217; before savings come An interim review earlier this year found that for every £1 the government invested in employment, about £25 was spent on benefits. An addition of the costs of the Neets crisis for the UK economy came to around £125bn every year.&nbsp; Under Milburn’s plans, mayors will be accountable for delivery through agreements while a new ‘Youth Opportunity Service’ would be created around the country. The service will connect young people to advisers, who will set up career plans.&nbsp; Milburn previously cautioned that, as a result of up-front investment, savings could take about a decade to emerge.&nbsp; He said his proposals formed the basis of a national ambition to bring the Neets rate from 13 per cent down to around five per cent by 2036.&nbsp; Pat McFadden, the work and pensions secretary, is set to respond to the full report on Tuesday afternoon. Shadow work and pensions secretary Helen Whately said the review was &#8220;too cautious on reversing&#8221; damage done by higher taxes and more regulation imposed on businesses. &#8220;Overall, it fails to bring down the welfare bill, proposes billions more in government spending and won&#8217;t make the slightest difference for young people stuck on welfare now &#8211; because nothing will happen for at least three years,&#8221; Whately said.
