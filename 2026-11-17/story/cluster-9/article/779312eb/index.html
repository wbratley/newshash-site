How credible is the reported Iran ‘dirty bomb’ threat to Washington DC?

Article text

New US intelligence suggests Iran is developing plans to detonate a radioactive “dirty bomb” in Washington, DC, aiming to take out senior Trump administration officials, the CNN network reports. Two officials, described by CNN as “familiar with the latest intelligence”, said the plot was still at the stage of investigating options rather than an imminent attack. However they said planning picked up by the US had “been very specific and includes locations and targets”. The White House told CNN it was “monitoring any and all potential threats, but at this time there are no credible threats to the homeland.” The report was greeted with some skepticism, and notably no other media outlet followed it with firsthand confirmation. One analyst called it “bogus” and speculated it had been put out by new White House Chief of Staff Stephen Miller. The news broke as Donald Trump left the country, via a refuelling stop in Alaska, en route to the Philippines and then a meeeting with Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin in Shenzhen, China. What CNN claimed CNN said the fresh intelligence showed that Tehran was now actively planning for potential attacks inside US territory, though there was no evidence they had yet decided to move forward. “Whether they actually have the personnel in place to execute is a matter of debate,” one of the sources told the network. Officials said that dirty bombs, which package radioactive materials into a conventional bomb, could be put together under instructions and would not necessarily require a trained operative. CNN said intelligence had “picked up Iranian chatter claiming that it has stashed bomb-making materials inside the United States”. Officials were divided over how credible this was but “broadly believe that it would be very easy, and is even likely, that Iran has already pre-positioned nuclear materials or chemical agents that could be laced onto a conventional weapon”. Iran said it “categorically rejects and unequivocally refutes” allegations that it was considering using a chemical weapon. Is the threat credible? The immediate reaction from security analysts to the CNN report was scepticism, with some on social media questioning how an imprecise method like a dirty bomb could be used to target specific individuals. Others recalled regular claims in the noughties that al-Qaeda was planning a dirty bomb attack in the US, which never eventuated. While the risk of a “dirty bomb” terror attack has long been considered, none has ever been successfully deployed. National security consultant Adam Silverman called it a “Stephen Miller special” designed to “create fear and a pretext” for renewed attacks on Iran. “The Iranians are not that stupid, they know exactly what the response would be,” he said. Counterterrorism expert Andy Vitek said the supposed plot “makes no sense and is utterly out of step with how Iran and the [Revolutionary Guard] have historically operated”. “The US and Israel have set fire to their international credibility with this [2026 war] debacle, and setting off a radiological weapon on US soil would give it all back.” In a press gaggle at the White House on Monday just before his departure, Trump was asked “Are you worried about Iranian drones targeting you personally?” He replied, jokingly, to the reporter: “I’m worried about you, because if they get me, you’re gone too”. The question was likely prompted by reports in recent days in the Israeli media of an alleged Iranian plot to target Trump, and top US officials, with drones. However these reports have been clouded by claims they were politically generated by allies of Benjamin Netanyahu in order to give him leverage to stay in power. Israel has been pushing the US to take fresh military action against Iran. The director of Israeli intelligence service Mossad was in DC last week, meeting senior Trump administration officials and the CIA director John Ratcliffe. ‘Assassins on the loose’ However there is evidence that Iran has ambitions to assassinate senior officials. “Iran’s regime has been hunting top US officials,” Republican congressman and senior member of the House Foreign Affairs Committee Joe Wilson told Congress last week, in a speech that the official Congressional Record titled “Iran’s assassins are on the loose”. “US enforcement has broken up other Iranian murder-for-hire plots against President Trump,” he said. “This month, the Iranian regime put up a billboard in Tehran announcing: We Will Kill Trump….When the regime in Tehran chants ‘Death to Israel, Death to America,’ they mean it.” The speech came after it emerged that US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has been moved from his Washington DC home to a military base after Iran-linked threats to his life. The White House’s new chief of staff, Stephen Miller, was also moved to a base in late 2025. Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth and Secretary of State Marco Rubio both reportedly live on military bases. Last month the Director of National Intelligence, Jay Clayton, wrote an unclassified memo to the Secretary of State Marco Rubio referring to “intelligence that Iran has substantially increased efforts to target US personnel”, including government officials in the Middle East and “certain other areas”, though it was interpreted to mainly refer to military and diplomatic personnel based in the region. Iran has previously denied plotting to attack officials and people on US soil. In March, a trained Iranian Revolutionary Guard operative, Asif Merchant, was convicted in a US court after being sent to the US to arrange for the assassination of a politician or government official. He got as far as making a $5,000 downpayment to two New York hitmen – but authorities were on to him and the ‘hitmen’ were undercover law enforcement. A wider threat In July last year, well before the 2026 conflict began in February, the US, UK and 12 other countries issued a joint statement on “the growing number of state threats from Iranian intelligence services”. It said Irainian intelligence services were attempting to “kill, kidnap and harass” people in Europe and North America, and were “increasingly collaborating with international criminal organisations to target journalists, dissidents, Jewish citizens, and current and former officials”. In recent weeks Iran has reportedly been debating how to make its threats of retaliation more credible, to deter the prospect of a renewed assault from the US and/or Israel. Options include attempted attacks on military bases in Europe and beyond, or a reconsideration of the country’s nuclear doctrine. Last week US prosecutors charged three men it alleged were linked to an Iran-backed militia plotting multiple attacks across Europe, and the US, including arson attacks in London and at a RAF base. Recently, attention has also focused on Iranian cyber attacks. In March, a cyber attack by a hacktivist group linked to Iranian intelligence “wiped” medical equipment orders to hospitals in the UK. Frank Cilluffo, a former Department of Homeland Security official who played a lead role in the White House’s immediate post-9/11 security overhaul, told Newsweek in August that Iran preferred “asymmetric” attacks, rather than confronting foes directly on a battlefield. “It can look for softer seams in our economy and critical infrastructure, impose costs far from the battlefield, and create uncertainty about when and where the next shoe may drop,” he said. Cyber attacks on water facilities in six US states earlier this year were attributed to Iranian cyber actors. US and Israeli strikes on Iran have killed Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, Iran’s Supreme Leader, as well as an estimated 40 Iranian officials, including senior military, Revolutionary Guard and intelligence figures.
