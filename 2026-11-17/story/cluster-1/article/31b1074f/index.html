The big problem with Robert Pattinson playing Bond

Article text

At last, after months – years even – of frenzied speculation over who could be the next James Bond, we have a name: Robert Pattinson. He is 007. Cue internet meltdown alongside euphoric sighs of relief; scenes of rejoicing alongside howls of despair. It all comes with the territory. Pattinson is not the obvious choice for James Bond. That was Jack Lowden, surely? Historically, the Bond franchise has cast only moderately famous men in the role, allowing them to become 007 without bringing too much in the way of baggage. But Pattinson, 40, is fully A-list, and certainly no stranger to franchises. He’s already been in Harry Potter, and Twilight, and Dune, and Batman, and so he might not be able to disappear into the role as invisibly as some of his predecessors. He is, of course, that most unusual breed of actor who can straddle popcorn ephemera and arthouse intensity with comparable ease. He was mesmerising, for example, in the deeply claustrophobic The Lighthouse alongside Willem Dafoe, while more recently was exquisitely wrought in The Drama, a strong contender for the best – and certainly most off-kilter – film of the year. But now, it’s popcorn ephemera all the way. Which is strange, when you consider that Pattinson doesn’t even seem to like franchises very much. He would often complain about his recurring role in Twilight, calling it “weird” and “strange”, and wondered if the films made any sense at all (though he has recently begun speaking a little more kindly about Twilight). The Bond producers will surely expect him to toe the line a little more faithfully. It’ll be fascinating to see if he does. For all the kudos that must come with becoming Bond – world fame; enormous wealth – the role might yet prove a poisoned chalice. Few film franchises command more column inches, or as much hyperbolic fan chatter. Daniel Craig – the most recent Bond – in ‘Skyfall’ (Photo: Francois Duhamel/Avalon) What’s more, all that arthouse intensity will have to be shelved, because this new appointment tends to require less acting, not more. James Bond can morph towards sensitivity and self-awareness as much as he wants in an enlightened 21st century, but he is still an alpha male, and still has a licence to kill. James Bond in a nutshell: run fast, shoot people. The extracurricular benefits remain, of course: a smorgasbord of readily available sex; a level of car insurance that permits him to drive an Aston Martin above the national speed limit; and an expense account at Rolex HQ. For the character himself, lovely stuff; for the actor that portrays him, one with Pattinson’s range? A little restricting, perhaps. A little too formulaic? Either way, the man has his work cut out for him, an ascent of Everest proportions. Ever since Barbara Broccoli and Michael G Wilson offloaded the world’s most valuable film franchise onto Amazon, certain quarters have been actively praying that this latest iteration will founder, if only to upset Jeff Bezos. But let’s face it, Bond is too big to fail, and we remain far too invested. The new film is in the hands of both a very capable director (Denis Villeneuve) and a super-smart screenwriter (Steven Knight). Pattinson himself, meanwhile, for all his maverick intent, all his leftfield appeal, possesses a very chiselled jaw. One thing right now is certain: 007 won’t make Pattinson any more interesting an actor, but we can only hope that he makes 007 more interesting. And if he does find himself helplessly drawn to franchises, they haven’t cast a new Doctor Who yet. And how about Voldemort in HBO’s Harry Potter? He’s good friends with Zendaya; perhaps she could put a word in for Spider-Man. Or he could revive the Jolly Green Giant for sweetcorn, or Mr Muscle, the drain unblocker? Seriously, the possibilities are endless.
