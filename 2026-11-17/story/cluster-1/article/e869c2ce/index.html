Robert Pattinson as James Bond is bad for Hollywood

Article text

Robert Pattinson will be a great Bond – but his casting is bad news for Hollywood In new stills released today it has been announced that Twilight and Batman actor Robert Pattinson will be the seventh actor to officially drink freezing cold vodka and a tiny bit of vermouth to play orphaned, traumatised, fashionable, perennially middle-aged James Bond.   Pattinson, who is 40 and rose to prominence through the five filmic adaptations of the Twilight Saga books, is a good choice. Firstly, he is a fine actor, with a range of indie projects under his belt, and an undefinable, hard-to-read quality that makes him alluring to Hollywood. You couldn’t quite pinpoint where he hangs out – the pub, a fine pile in the Hollywood Hills or a grungy gig – and it is that indie-to-mainstream crossover appeal has helped him sustain a career this long, developing the sort of CV former 007 producer Barbara Broccoli prefers (Daniel Craig, too, had been an indie darling before he picked up the holster).  If you were to rank his undefinable, draw-you-in qualities, those smokey eyes that somehow seem both blue and brown but give nothing away would be at the top of the list. Robert Pattinson is good for James Bond, bad for Hollywood But Pattinson’s casting is less good for Hollywood. James Bond is surely the last role that could rocket an unheard of actor to the A-List, and not casting a burgeoning name is a missed opportunity.&nbsp; These days, it is incredibly hard to birth new stars. It was not this way in the 1990s when Pierce Brosnan was cast, and to some extent with Daniel Craig. Back then, putting a new face on the cover of The Sunday Times Culture magazine could make someone famous. I recall the former editor of that magazine telling me he’d once argued to give the cover to a little-known actor called Tom Cruise, and we all know how that went. But journalism doesn’t have a tenth of that power anymore – put someone on the front of a weekend supplement and they might generate a few hundred Instagram followers, but not a glittering Blockbuster career. And then there are the nepo babies – the Anais Gallaghers, Hero Fiennes Tiffins and Rafferty Laws – clogging up the talent pipeline, making it even harder for new actors who don’t have famous parents to break through.  The cinema also used to be a good platform for rising actors, but with fewer people going to the flicks at the weekend, and fewer films being made, producers are choosing safety by relying on bankable stars rather than newcomers (hello, R Patz). The death of the rom-com is a signifier of how people are mostly going to the cinema to see &#8216;event&#8217; films these days, like new Batman, Spider-Man or James Bond films. The power of franchises at the box office was proven again recently when Tom Cruise&#8217;s latest film, Digger, became one of the biggest box office disasters of the decade. Cruise&#8217;s casting alone couldn&#8217;t drum up interest from the public, illustrating how big stars can&#8217;t always guarantee box office return without the weight of franchises like Mission Impossible behind them. One man who can, though, is James Bond. Ian Fleming&#8217;s spy could remedy all of these problems: I&#8217;ll put it simply: cast anyone to play him, and the cinemas will sell out. I mean, not literally anyone, but cast someone good, and people will go.  This clearly doesn&#8217;t play into the interests of new Bond owners Amazon – they just want a big name because a big name sells, especially after investing £1bn in new Pinewood studio expansion – but it would be good for the pipeline of cinema. Creating an exciting new star is high-on impossible these days – and Bond choosing not to take a punt on an upcoming name is a shame for Hollywood.
