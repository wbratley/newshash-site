Suffolk fire live: Hundreds of firefighters battle 'unprecedented' wildfire near nuclear plant A wildfire is raging in Dunwich Heath, Suffolk where evacuations have been ordered - the blaze is close to three nuclear plant sites, and has burned an area the size of 210 football pitches Hundreds of firefighters are battling through the night to contain a massive wildfire in Dunwich Heath, Suffolk, that has devasted a huge nature reserve and threatened a nearby nuclear power plant. Temperatures soared into the mid-30s in southern England on Thursday, burning an area the size of 210 football pitches in a National Trust reserve just a few miles away from Sizewell B - the UK's largest nuclear power plant. Some 200 caravans were evacuated this morning, with holidaymakers woken up by banging on their doors. Eighteen fire engines and more than 100 firefighters are now at the scene, with authorities saying they expect crews to be battling the blaze for the next two days. There is also concern for wildlife in the area, after nature reserve RSPB Minsmere announcing its closure this morning. RSPB Suffolk area manager Adam Rowlands said the land was home to rare wildlife including nightjars - and it could take "more than a decade" for the habitat to recover. Prime Minister Andy Burnham said the fire service will have “all of the support” they need in tackling the blaze. The National Trust has called for urgent adaptation to climate extremes – and warned the Government not to back down on action to cut emissions – as the UK battles the latest wildfires brought on by hot dry conditions, and half of England and Wales are now in drought. The charity’s intervention comes in the wake of 200 blazes nationwide in the past fortnight, include large areas of National Trust landscapes such as on the Isle of Wight and currently at Dunwich Heath in Suffolk. Hilary McGrady, director-general at the National Trust said: “We’ve all been watching in horror as our neighbours in Europe are battling the most extreme fires. “Fires like these might once have seemed unthinkable in Europe and the UK – but that certainty is gone.” She called for urgent adaptation to the new conditions, including building homes fit for the future, creating wetter and more resilient landscapes, cooling towns and cities and helping communities to prepare for the changing weather. “It means taking steps now to protect our health, our heritage and our way of life,” she urged. “But while we must adapt, we mustn’t accept these conditions as “normal” and become desensitised to the underlying issue while there is still time to change course. “Every nation has to tackle the problem at source – and that means leaving fossil fuels in the ground. Andy Burnham and his government must not stall in their response. The UK has responded to enormous challenges over the last century, and with enough will and strong leadership, it can do so again,” she urged. The UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA) has released the following health advice to locals. Residents in areas affected by the smoke should stay indoors and keep their doors and windows closed. Motorists who have to travel through the smoke should keep windows closed, turn off air conditioning and keep their air vents closed. Some of the substances present in smoke can irritate the lining of the air passages, the skin and the eyes. Respiratory symptoms can include coughing and wheezing, breathlessness and chest pain, if you experience any of these contact your GP or call NHS 111. Chemicals in the smoke can worsen existing health problems like asthma so people are encouraged to carry their inhaler with them. Due to hot weather, we advise extra precautions: - Draw curtains to limit direct sunlight heating-up internal surfaces. - Use fans to recirculate air within the house to help keep cool. - Keep hydrated with cool drinks. - Set any air conditioning (preferably fitted with a HEPA filter) to recirculate mode. - Once the smoke has moved away: consider opening doors and windows to allow properties to cool down, being mindful of changing conditions. The smoke may have moved away but it could just be a brief respite and could return. It is important to listen to ongoing public advice about ventilating your property. Drought has now been declared across the whole of Wales, with this month on course to be the driest July in Wales since records began 190 years ago, Natural Resources Wales (NRW) said. North Wales and the Upper Severn were given drought status last week and, after continued low rainfall and high temperatures, this has now been extended to include the whole of the country, the devolved Government agency said. The majority of rivers across Wales are exceptionally low, and the dry conditions are leading to river beds drying out, distressing fish, causing algal blooms, and raising concerns from farmers about crop growth, the body said. There were also concerns about grazing conditions and levels of drinking water for livestock, it added. Rhian Thomas, Sustainable Water and Nature Manager at NRW, said: “The intensity of the heat and the exceptional lack of rainfall means this is a rapidly changing picture in Wales. “Our teams are closely monitoring forecasts and river flows and responding to increasing environmental concerns and incidents across Wales. “The toll on the environment, our land and agriculture is clear to see, with many of our teams still dealing with the aftermath of extensive wildfires which have devastated forestry, grassland and special habitats. “With no strong indications for notable rainfall in the forecasts, and risks of further heatwaves over the summer, it’s more important than ever that we all use water wisely.
