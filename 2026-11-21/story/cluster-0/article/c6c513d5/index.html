What is in the UK-EU deal? How it will affect travel, food prices and young people

Log in The UK and the EU have clinched a deal designed to undo some of the harms of Brexit, at Andy Burnham’s crunch summit in Brussels. Both sides will now work towards implementing a youth mobility scheme, and agreements on food and emissions trading, although talks will continue on some areas. The prime minister has slammed the current arrangements – negotiated by the Conservatives when they were in power – as not “good enough for Britain”. He hailed the new agreement as “a good day for Britain and ... for Europe” and a “win-win.” Mr Burnham hopes the deal can herald the beginning of closer UK-EU ties, that could even include trying to rejoin the bloc. Youth mobility The scheme will enable young people aged between 18 and 31 to work, study, au pair, volunteer or travel in each other’s territories for up to three years. It is designed to give them “the opportunity to broaden their horizons, experience new cultures, learn languages, and gain new personal and professional experiences”. Earlier this week it was still to be decided many would benefit. The UK wanted a cap of between 50,000 and 80,000 places, amid fears of claims of betrayal and a return to freedom of movement from Brexiteers, while the EU was pushing for at least 100,000, which is the final number. However, young people will to wait until January 2030, in part because work permits are actually under the control of the 27 national governments that make up the EU, not Brussels. Food and drink exports The agreement includes removing border checks on food and drink imports in exchange for the UK aligning with EU sanitary and phytosanitary regulations by March 2028. It should mean exports of fresh sausages, burgers, shellfish from domestic waters and seed potatoes to the EU can resume. The UK government claims the food and agriculture deal will add up to £5.1 billion a year to the economy, cutting red tape by removing the need for certificates and border checks for products crossing between the UK and EU. For individual businesses, it will remove costs of between £25 and £200 for different certificates and at least £127.60 per load in inspection fees, according to official documents released alongside the agreement. The agreement also allows British pet owners to obtain a multi-use pet passport valid for travel to the EU, which ministers say will make taking pets on holiday easier and cheaper. Energy The UK wants to join the EU’s electricity market by 2027, which may mean taxpayers will have to help fund projects to build roads, airports and other infrastructure across poorer EU states. Linking the emissions trading regime, which will protect British exporters from facing a carbon tax when supplying customers in the EU, could add a £3.8 billion annual boost, officials claim. Cheaper tuition fees for EU students at UK universities This is still a bone of contention. The EU is pushing for their students to pay cheaper, UK fees at British institutions. Both sides have agreed to enter negotiations on an agreement, to conclude “within six months”. The aim would be to increase “opportunities for our students to study in each other’s territories”. Security, defence and development The UK and the EU have agreed to “enhance our cooperation on security and defence”, to help support Ukraine, tackle hybrid threats, respond to crises, and boost European defence industrial and capability development. They say that “standing together in the face of increasing hybrid threats to Europe, we will increase action to prepare for and respond to threats and work to impose costs on hostile actors, strengthen vulnerable partners, and reinforce our cooperation on democratic resilience.” There is also talk of a new Memorandum of Understanding on Health Security, to prevent and respond to serious health threats. Migration The UK and EU have agreed to enhance the information they exchange capabilities to better detect, apprehend and prosecute criminals. They will also “take forward joint activities” to cooperate against organised immigration crime, in a bid to reduce small boat crossings. Goodwill and trust? The PM hopes this agreement will be just a launchpad for his plans for much closer ties with the EU, including potentially going “all the way”. He still faces further talks with the EU to land some of the agreement's more sticky elements, including on cuts to tuition fees for EU students at UK universities. But a key question is - is he leaving Brussels with the trust and goodwill of EU partners, which will be vital for any new arrangements going forward. The potential prize is huge. Mr Burnham has not ruled out that the next Labour manifesto could include a pledge to rejoin the EU – a move that could write him into the history books.
