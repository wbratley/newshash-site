What the Brexit reset deal really means for you – from food prices to visas

Article text

Andy Burnham has secured a landmark Brexit reset deal with the EU. Speaking in Brussels on Friday, the Prime Minister said the deal would be “good for working people, good for Britain and good for Europe”. According to the Treasury, the agreement will deliver a £17bn boost to the economy. Burnham said it represented the “single biggest growth measure that we could take in this Parliament”, while broadening “horizons” for young people and helping families with the cost of living. At the summit, he and a team of ministers met Ursula von der Leyen, the President of the EU Commission, and António Costa, the President of the European Council. Here are the key aspects of the agreement – and what exactly it means for you. The price of food and drink The food and drink deal is the big win for the UK – and it could directly impact your supermarket shop. Since Britain left the EU, British producers and retailers have had to deal with costly red tape to export to the bloc. This requires companies to fill out hundreds of pages of paperwork for every lorry bound for the EU to demonstrate that it complies with single market rules, with vets also having to check, hand-sign and stamp each document. On Friday, Burnham highlighted how costs can go up to £1,400 for a single shipment of salmon and that the extra admin had resulted in agri-food exports to the EU falling by more than 30 per cent since Brexit. By reaching a food and drink deal “the vast majority of movements of animals, animal products, plants and plant products” will be able to take place “without the certificates or controls that are currently required”. It will mean that British products that are currently banned from the EU – including fresh sausages, burgers, seed potatoes and molluscs – can once again be exported there. The joint statement says the aim is for this to apply from 1 March 2028. Burnham said that this food and drink deal would ‘help’ with the cost of living (Photo: Getty) Crucially, both sides have said the deal – known as a sanitary and phytosanitary (SPS) agreement – will cut costs for businesses and be positive for UK food and drink prices. It will also cut transport times and reduce the amount of food being wasted by delays. Von der Leyen said it would allow “easier trade and lower cost” for businesses and “more choice and more affordable food” for consumers. Burnham added: “For people who are worried about the cost of the weekly shop, this deal will help”. Under the SPS agreement, Britain will be required to update its own food and drink rules in line with EU regulations, in a process known as “dynamic alignment”. The UK will have an “early decision-shaping role on EU rules in development”, but will still have to implement rules it has not devised itself or may not agree with. The deal is also good news for British pet owners wanting to take their animals to Europem because it will allow them to travel with a multi-use pet passport rather than paying for an animal health certificate each time they visit. Visas for young people The UK and the EU have agreed to set up a new Youth Experience Scheme (YES), which is good news for young people who want to experience life abroad. In total, 100,000 people from the EU – under the age of 32 – will be able to live in the UK for up to three years and “engage in activities such as work, studies, au-pairing, volunteering, or travelling”. The same number of young people from the UK will be able to travel in the opposite direction, and do the same things. Burnham said the scheme would lift the “life chances” of people for whom Brexit had “limited their horizons”. A joint statement released by the EU and UK said each would “aim to apply this agreement no later than 2028 and endeavour to ensure that opportunities under the scheme will be available to our young people no later than January 2030”. The visas will be distributed between EU member states on the basis of population size. This applies for both EU participants heading to the UK, and outbound Britons heading to the EU. UK citizens will apply to a particular member state and will subsequently be able to switch to a different country, staying in a total of three member states over a three-year period. Britain and EU member states will be able to restrict what jobs participants can get. However, both sides have said they intend to offer “broad labour market access when the scheme opens”, with the majority of EU countries not planning to impose any work restrictions on UK citizens. Stability for carbon prices Another major agreement on Friday was the linking of the UK and EU Emissions Trading Systems (ETS). These are the systems via which the bloc and the UK can limit the amount of carbon that power plants and industry can be emitted each year. Companies subject to the scheme buy and trade allowances so they can emit carbon above these caps. The UK used to be part of the EU’s scheme but left it after Brexit and has since set up its own system. Linking the two will exempt British exporters from new EU carbon border taxes that Brussels is planning to introduce on high-carbon products, such as steel and cement. Combining with the EU’s much bigger ETS could mean more stable carbon prices for British companies, making it easier for them to develop long-term plans for decarbonisation. Supporters say it could reduce costs for both EU and UK consumers. However, the UK currently has a lower carbon price, so in the short term it could potentially increase prices for British businesses. The cost of tuition fees The biggest unresolved area relates to tuition fees. The EU has been pushing for the UK Government to lower the fees that European students have to pay to study at British universities. EU students currently pay the same fees as international students, which can reach up to £38,000 per year for undergraduate students – far higher than the home costs they were charged before Brexit. Brussels has asked for a return to home fees, which currently stand at £9,790 a year for students in England, but are due to rise to £10,050 in 2027-28. The UK and EU were unable to agree on reducing fees in time for the summit, so they have given themselves an extra six months. The biggest unresolved area relates to tuition fees (Photo: Getty) The joint statement says both sides will “work towards a supplementing agreement” on “tuition fees for EU students in the UK and for UK students in the EU”. It adds: “The agreement will apply to all students from the other party irrespective of the immigration pathway. The aim is to increase opportunities for students to study in each other’s territories. We will conclude the negotiations within six months from the date of the summit.” Von der Leyen said she was “confident” the sides would find a solution on fees on Friday, to which Burnham replied: “Me too.” Because it is a reciprocal agreement, the eventual deal will lower the fees paid by British students attending European universities. The fees charged by higher education institutions varies significantly across different countries in the EU, so it is not yet clear what this will look like in practice.
