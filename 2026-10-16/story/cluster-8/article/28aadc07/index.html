Severe drought declared in parts of southern England

Article text

Severe drought declared in parts of southern England - Published Severe drought has been declared across parts of southern England after months of low rainfall. Parts of Cornwall, Devon, Dorset, Somerset, East Sussex and West Sussex are affected by the announcement from the Environment Agency, which followed a review of water resources and drought conditions across England by the National Drought Group. Water supplies have been significantly impacted by high demand following the hottest summer on record. The government said customer supplies would be uninterrupted and declaring severe drought "ensures proactive steps are taken to safeguard water before any disruption occurs". The government's plans include: - Ministers instructing water companies to conduct daily monitoring of reservoir and river levels and tougher water quality monitoring - The Environment Agency to increase compliance checks on businesses that withdraw water from natural sources to ensure they are only taking permitted quantities of water The government said the change to severe drought status had been prompted by specific concerns for two major reservoirs, Ardingly in West Sussex and Wimbleball in Somerset. The latest figures, external for Wimbleball Reservoir on Exmoor show it is at 14.9% capacity, while Ardingly is 19.4% full, external. Other major reservoirs are also low, including Roadford in Devon at 36.8%, Burrator at 33.6%, Colliford in Cornwall at 35% and Stithians at 21.9%. The Environment Agency has classed Colliford and Wimbleball as having exceptionally low levels, external. Wimbleball serves about 300,000 properties across east Devon, north Devon, Somerset, west Dorset and West Somerset. Ardingly Reservoir serves 280,000 customers across East and West Sussex. The Environment Agency said it expected reservoir storage to be about 75% at this time of the year. In its latest drought report, external it said reservoir storage had continued to fall at most reservoirs in England. The agency has granted South East Water and South West Water drought orders giving them permission to cut the amount of water sent from reservoirs to rivers by millions of litres per day. Mark Rice, the Environment Agency area director for Devon, Cornwall and the Isles of Scilly, said "the importance of this move is that it pushes the day when further restrictions are needed, so they won't be needed at all across the winter period and that we're able to refill the reservoirs". Southern Water also applied for an order to restrict usage in Hampshire and the Isle of Wight. "Recovery depends on both rainfall and how much water we use. We cannot change the weather, but water companies can act now. "We expect them to follow their drought plans and take the steps needed to secure public water supplies. "We are also asking everyone to use less water. That will leave more in our rivers and the environment, helping farmers and protecting wildlife and nature." The government said the South East and South West had experienced their driest April to September in more than a century, impacting reservoir levels in that area. Environment Secretary Dame Angela Eagle said: "This level of drought is something we have not experienced for decades, so this government is stepping up our support and planning. "We are working closely with water companies to protect public supplies in the areas affected, and I'd continue to urge everyone to think about how they can use water wisely over the coming weeks." A hosepipe ban has been in force across Devon, Cornwall, the Isles of Scilly and parts of Dorset and Somerset since 8 August and there is no definite end date. South West Water said a "prolonged period of hot and dry weather" along with "continuous high demand for water" was behind the ban. South East Water's operations director Douglas Whitfield said standpipes remained part of the company's drought plan but were unlikely to be needed. Whitfield said a wet winter had been forecast but timings were not clear and the firm was making any adjustments it could to protect supplies. Whitfield said South East Water, which supplies more than two million customers, said 92 repair crews were out across Sussex and Kent tackling leaks to minimise loss from the network. In an email sent to customers South East Water said it was optimising water pressure across the county and had more than doubled the number of teams tackling leaks in the region, increasing from seven to 16. It said the number of leaks in the affected area had fallen from 181 to 97 and that more than 1,300 leaks had been repaired since 1 April. Wessex Water, which supplies customers from Wimbleball, introduced its first hosepipe ban in 50 years in August and said the restrictions would remain in place for the foreseeable future. The water company said it had increased leak repairs, moved water into the area from more resilient sources and adjusted water pressure in some communities to conserve supplies and urged customers to continue using water carefully. Will Willoughby, director of water supply at Wessex Water, said: "We saw record levels of demand through the network - and it sustained above the 400 million litres a day for 48 days, which is far and away the toughest year that we've seen in terms of that height in demand." He said customer impact "should be none" but encouraged customers to continue to be mindful about water usage. Paul Tompkins, deputy president of The National Farmers' Union (NFU) said it had been a "torrid year for farmers", where the conditions proved "very difficult" to harvest crops, provide water for livestock, and a "potential dry winter could have implications for next year's crops".
