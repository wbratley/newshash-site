John Healey poised to raise taxes on banks in budget

Article text

John Healey is poised to tax Britain’s banks in his 28 October budget, to help offset the damage inflicted on the public finances by war in the Middle East, the Guardian understands. The chancellor is in Bangkok, where the International Monetary Fund has praised the UK’s progress towards balancing the books, urging him to “stay the course”. To keep the Treasury’s deficit reduction plans on track in the face of rising government borrowing costs, the Guardian understands Healey has decided to tap the financial sector for additional revenue. One option understood to be under discussion – reversing cuts to the bank surcharge made by Jeremy Hunt – would raise upwards of £2bn a year. Lenders have been logging strong earnings on the back of rising interest rates, with the UK’s four largest lenders – HSBC, NatWest, Barclays and Lloyds Banking Group – alone having generated £200bn in pre-tax profits over the past five years. Thinktanks and trade unions have repeatedly urged Healey to tax Britain’s banks more heavily, an option considered but rejected by his predecessor Rachel Reeves, who was wary of deterring future investment. Paul Nowak, the general secretary of the Trades Union Congress (TUC), said: “Britain’s big four banks are making a billion pounds a week, while bonuses have hit a post-crash record. These are bumper profits, driven by high interest rates and households’ mortgage misery. It’s time to make sure banks pay their fair share.” Speaking to journalists in Bangkok, Healey praised the banks as crucial to supporting economic growth. “People too often see them as big, distant financial institutions, when they are hardwired into the economic fabric of this country and our future prospects,” he said. But he is nevertheless believed to be minded to increase the surcharge banks pay on their profits, over and above corporation tax. Industry figures believe that will probably mean raising the surcharge back to 8%, from 3% at present. The UK’s cost of borrowing has risen sharply in recent weeks, alongside that of other big economies, as bond markets have responded to the prospect of higher inflation as a result of the Middle East conflict. Analysts have suggested that could as much as halve the £24bn cushion Reeves had built up against Labour’s fiscal rules at the time of the spring forecast in March. Healey is understood to be willing to accept a narrower “headroom”, but is also keen to convince bond markets he is wedded to deficit reduction. Bank bosses have been lobbying intensively against tax rises since narrowly escaping higher levies during last year’s budget. UK bosses of lenders including HSBC, Lloyds, NatWest, Barclays and Santander met Healey last week but were given no reassurance that bank tax hikes had been ruled out. Their lobby groups – including UK Finance and TheCityUK – wrote to Healey days later, saying that on top of “weakening investors confidence and damaging UK attractiveness”, higher taxes would force them to move staff and business out of the country. That echoed warnings by the chief executive of JP Morgan, Jamie Dimon, who said in May he could scrap plans for a new £3bn London headquarters if the UK government became hostile towards banks. News of the tower – which will house more than half of the US investment bank’s 23,000 UK staff – was came days after Reeves spared the sector further tax rises in her 2025 autumn budget. One industry source said banks were resigned to a rise in the surcharge. They believe Healey is minded to reverse Hunt’s cut – taking it back to 8% – but not go further. Such a move would raise £2.25bn a year, according to analysis by the TUC. Another industry source said they were caught off guard by the change in messaging, having come just hours after Healey hailed banks as a “jewel” in the crown of the British economy. “We’re surprised to see this following earlier comments which appeared to be more supportive of the sector.” When asked about how much the banking industry might be able to absorb in terms of tax rises, the head of policy at TheCityUK, John Godfrey, said banks “do not want to get into debating the menu of taxation. The fact is that we don’t want to eat in that restaurant.” Healey has also been “listening to representations” from specialist banks, including Revolut and Shawbrook, that want the profit threshold at which the surcharge kicks in to be raised from £100m to £500m. One government source said Labour was mindful of the UK needing to offer a positive environment for startup banks, especially the lending they offered to small businesses. The TUC has lobbied for a much more punitive increase, to 16%, saying that could raise £24bn in tax revenues over the next four years. Healey said he wanted his first budget as chancellor to give “breathing space” to both consumers and businesses in the face of rising energy prices. There will be little additional funding for other Whitehall departments, however, which he stressed would have to stick to the budgets Reeves set out at her spending review in June 2025. “It’s important that they do manage within what they’ve got, and it’s part of the job,” Healey said. “You have to reprioritise if you want to do fresh things, or more.” The Institute for Fiscal Studies has said Reeves’s plans could result in real-terms spending cuts for some departments in the later years of the spending review period. The Conservative leader, Kemi Badenoch, used a visit to a financial services firm in Edinburgh on Thursday to caution against further banking tax rises, saying the sector already faced a higher effective tax rate than in many other big global financial centres. Andrew Griffith, the shadow chancellor, said: “Britain needs growth. But a raid on the financial services sector will stifle it. Labour should be backing businesses and wealth-creators, not making their lives even harder. Rather than hiking taxes yet again, Labour should cut spending.” As well as an energy support package for households and businesses, Healey’s first budget is expected to set out more details of plans to devolve income tax to mayoralties, and explain how the government will fully fund the four-year defence investment plan announced by Keir Starmer before he stepped down as prime minister. Healey is expected to duck the longer-term question of when the UK will spend 3% of gross domestic product on defence, the issue over which he resigned as defence secretary in June, contributing to Starmer’s downfall.
