Government backs welfare shake-up to prevent 'lost generation'

Article text

Government backs welfare shake-up to prevent 'lost generation' - Published The government has backed plans by former minister Alan Milburn for sweeping reforms to prevent "losing a generation" of young people to a life on benefits. Milburn proposes spending billions on tailored support for under 25s not in employment, education or training (Neets), to be part-funded by cuts to some benefits. He claims his plan, which also proposes tax breaks for firms and major education reforms, will get 440,000 young people into jobs by 2036. Prime Minister Andy Burnham said he endorsed the "principles" of the Milburn review and was confident he could persuade Labour MPs to back legislation to implement its measures. The PM told Sky News he would set out a full response to the review next month, after he had received a separate report into Personal Independence Payments (Pips) by Social Security Minister Sir Stephen Timms. But he added: "I have never in my years in politics seen a wholesale, whole government approach to supporting the next generation. "That is what this represents, and I am absolutely confident that I can take this vision and get people behind it, indeed get the country behind it, because we will not place Britain in the position I want it to be in if we have a generation of young people growing up without hope for the future." The government has made Milburn the head of a new new youth opportunities unit to deliver on his recommendations. Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch said her party agreed with Milburn's "diagnosis" but claimed Labour had "made the problem much worse for young people" by increasing employers' National Insurance contributions and the youth minimum wage. There are few signs so far that Burnham will face a backlash from his own MPs of the scale that blocked his predecessor Sir Keir Starmer's planned £5bn welfare cuts last year. But some left-wing Labour MPs and the Unison trade union have warned proposals to cut health-related benefits will drive some young people into poverty. Work and Pensions Secretary Pat McFadden told MPs that "in future" the welfare state should be judged "not just by a snapshot of people's monthly incomes" but "but by the story of people's lives, and by their ability to achieve their full potential". McFadden, who was flanked by Burnham and Chancellor John Healey during his statement in the Commons, said the government rejected claims that young people were "snowflakes and shirkers". The million who were not in work or training had been robbed of the "prospect of a good future" and that was something that demanded "much more" than a conventional government response, he told MPs. He said the government would publish draft proposals on welfare reform early next year, with legislation to follow. In his wide-ranging 250 page report, external, Milburn proposes: - An "entirely new benefits system" for under-25s, which would end the health-top up of Universal Credit and Personal Independence Payments (Pip), the main disability benefit in England and Wales - Instead, a new "youth allowance" would be set at the basic rate of Universal Credit for under-25s, with a higher rate for those living independently - A reduction in employers' National Insurance costs for 21- to 24-year-olds, starting in the retail and hospitality sectors, meaning employing those young people would be cheaper - A huge expansion in post-18 college places and a legal right to work, education or training - A new, locally-led Youth Opportunity Service in England to give young people one named advisor and a personalised participation plan - Primary and secondary schools required to identify and support pupils at risk of becoming Neet, and schools obliged to offer work experience The report calls for the government to spend around £5.8bn in total by 2036-37 on extra mental health and employment support. And it urges ministers to "kickstart the reform programme" next year, with an initial £500m, from "repriotising" existing spending. - At a glance: Key proposals from Milburn's Neets report - Published18 hours ago - Why Milburn's Neets reforms will only save £200m a year by 2036 - Published16 hours ago - Where are the most jobs for 18-24 year-olds? - Published17 hours ago Milburn has said his plans are not about saving money - and his report says the government will not see overall savings from reduced benefit bills and higher tax receipts until 2036/27, when it will claw back £200m. But by then, it assumes the share of 16–24-year-olds that are Neet will have gone down from 13% - one of the highest in Europe - to 5%, the lowest in the UK since records began. Launching his report earlier on Tuesday, Milburn said "half measures will not do" because "the scale of the problem is just too big for that". He added: "Britain is at risk of losing a generation of young people, not because they lack talent, not because they lack ambition, not because they do not want to work. "This is not a crisis of aspiration; it is a crisis of opportunity." 'Flimsy sticking plaster' Milburn's report has no specific blueprint for Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland, where education, skills, health and local government are devolved. He said the UK and devolved governments "should agree common definitions, a shared set of headline outcomes and a regular joint review of progress". Scotland's Education Secretary Màiri McAllan said: "Where there is opportunity to collaborate with the UK government and others for the benefit of young people in Scotland, we will take it." But she argued many of the reforms recommended for England, such as stronger vocational training, were already in place in Scotland. A spokesperson for the Welsh government said: "We stand ready to work with the UK government to ensure Wales benefits fully from any UK-wide measures that improve life chances through access to rewarding work." Reform UK leader Nigel Farage said he agreed "with the general drift of the Milburn report, but it's too little and too slow". "We have a fundamental problem in Britain," he added. "Too many people are being encouraged to go on benefits, and training and apprenticeship schemes that take far too long." Lib Dem treasury spokesperson Daisy Cooper said if the government wanted to get people into work, it should back high street firms "that give many their first job", scrap the "unfair jobs tax" and negotiate a new growth and defence partnership with the EU. Green Party leader Zack Polanski described Milburn's proposals as a "flimsy sticking plaster" that intend to "slash support for disabled young people while subsidising corporations to hire cheap labour".
