How plan to tackle youth unemployment would require a 'whole system reset' and will cost the UK billions

Article text

How plan to tackle youth unemployment would require a 'whole system reset' and will cost the UK billions The plan to tackle youth unemployment proposed yesterday would require a 'whole system reset' across the public sector and cost billions of pounds a year – yet would save little money over a decade. Former minister Alan Milburn urged Andy Burnham to come up with a 'multi-billion pound investment', saying: 'We have to put our money where our mouth is.' He warned that without 'radical reforms', as many as 1.27 million under-25s will be classed as Neet – not in employment, education or training – in five years. But it is unclear if the Prime Minister will find the money required, which includes a demand for a £500 million 'down payment' as soon as next year. The report says net savings – of just £200 million – will not arrive until 2036-37. Reform's Treasury spokesman Robert Jenrick wrote on X: 'Alan Milburn's £200million savings by 2036 is the equivalent of just five hours of welfare spending. That's about the time it takes to download Grand Theft Auto VI.' Although Mr Milburn's goal is to reach 'Neet Zero', his measures are only forecast to halve the rate from 13 per cent currently – equivalent to 981,000 people aged 16 to 24 across the UK – to 6.8 per cent in 2036. These are the key points in his 278-page report. The report says a major driver in the crisis is the 'first rung on the career ladder' becoming harder for young people to reach, with youth employment rates falling by 4.3 per cent – equivalent to 330,000 people – between December 2022 and the end of last year. Former minister Alan Milburn has urged Andy Burnham to come up with a 'multi-billion pound investment' for youth unemployment It is scathing about the cumulative impact of Labour's policies including increases to the minimum wage, National Insurance (NI) hikes for employers and new protections under the Employment Rights Act. Wage costs for young people aged between 18 and 20 have soared by 65 per cent over the past five years while the NI rate has increased and the threshold fallen, adding £800 annually to the cost of a full-time worker. 'There is nothing progressive about pricing young people out of work,' the review states. Mr Milburn rejects a complete reversal of Rachel Reeves' changes, instead proposing a 'five-year glide path to lower national insurance costs for workers aged 21 to 24'. When 'fiscal conditions allow', he says, the point at which retail and hospitality bosses pay NI for 21 to 24-year-olds should double from the current £5,000 threshold to £10,000. This would help save the Saturday job, as a young shop worker could do 15 hours a week without their employer paying NI. The report also urges caution on Labour's manifesto pledge to bring the minimum wage for 18 to 20-year-olds into line with the National Living Wage, saying the Low Pay Commission quango should 'judge the pace of convergence against the critical goal of protecting youth employment'. And it warns of the 'unintended consequences' of Angela Rayner's crackdown on zero-hours contracts, urging ministers to consider the 'potential impact on youth employment' of rules still under consideration. Mr Milburn calls on ministers to introduce a Youth Opportunity Test so that tax changes, spending decisions and new regulations take into account the impact on the next generation. Mr Milburn's report warns of the 'unintended consequences' of Angela Rayner's crackdown on zero-hours contracts Launching his report at Google's offices in central London, he also took a swipe at Sir Keir Starmer's failed attempt to cut sickness benefits last year, saying: 'It was just a fiscal grab.' Too many pupils feel they do not belong in school, the report warns, with persistent absence two-thirds higher than before the pandemic. 'Technology has brought huge benefits, but the amount of childhood lived through a screen raises legitimate concerns about sleep, relationships, physical activity, attention and wellbeing,' it states. It says problems start when children are toddlers, calling for extra support for parents through 'family hubs' and a new target for 90 per cent of reception age pupils to be ready for school rather than 75 per cent currently. To identify those at risk of becoming Neets, there should be a statutory writing, numeracy and speaking 'diagnostic check' for pupils in Key Stage 3. Mr Milburn says schools should be measured on whether or not they prepare pupils for work, with a new performance measure of where they go next in place as soon as 2028. And the report calls for a near-doubling of apprenticeships, from 172,000 last year to 270,000 in a decade's time. More than half of Neets say they have a health condition and disability rates have more than doubled in the past 15 years. There has been a 'sharp' rise in the number with mild mental health conditions, with Covid, 'the rise of toxic social media' and loneliness all playing a part. The report says young people should get 'immediate access' to help with the NHS providing a new 'digital front door'. Rather than having to wait for a referral or diagnosis, they should be able to get online support including access to talking therapies or a 'brief' course of counselling. Private ADHD clinics, whose business model is 'cash for diagnosis,' should lose their NHS funding. As soon as 2027, responsibility for signing fit notes should be moved away from GPs who currently declare that almost all they see are deemed 'not fit for work'. The number of young people receiving incapacity benefits such as the Personal Independence Payment (PIP) has more than doubled to about 250,000 since 2012. 'Staggeringly, the number of young people claiming PIP is expected to quadruple over just a quarter of a century from 2013 to 2037,' the report says. Yet the growth in benefits 'far outweighs' changes in ill-health among young people. Mr Milburn insists that 'crude cuts' do not work, as leaving young people destitute will create costs in 'health, housing or criminal justice' instead. But he admits his proposals for a completely new benefits system for the under-25s will not be pain-free. 'They could mean some people get less financial support at the beginning of their benefit claim,' the report says. Instead of being able to claim universal credit health top-up or PIP, young people will get a new Youth Allowance, but there will also be help for those who can never work. As well as a guarantee of support, the plan also rests on an 'opportunity guarantee' from the state. A Youth Opportunity Service one-stop-shop will offer everything from a 'government-subsidised job', where employers get £3,000 for every young person they recruit, to further education places, volunteering or a cadetship with the Armed Forces. As a 'backstop', if a Neet person still cannot get work 'the state should secure and pay for a real paid six-month job with an employer'. Within 14 days a young person should be able to meet a named adviser in person, within four weeks have a personalised Participation Plan and within four months 'something substantive should be under way'.
