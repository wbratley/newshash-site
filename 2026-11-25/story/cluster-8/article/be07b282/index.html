Britain has gone off sex! A third of young adults are now virgins, while the average Briton is intimate just twice a month - and tech-obsessed Gen Z are to blame... as once-in-a-decade report uncovers UK sex habits Britain is in the grip of a sex recession with the youth of today the least intimate in a generation, striking findings show. Four in ten women aged between 16 and 24 have never had sex, researchers say, while more than a quarter of men of the same age are also virgins. It means around one in three 16 to 24-year-olds have never engaged in sex, a drastic jump from roughly one in five between 1990 and 2010. The data, which lays bare the sex lives of nearly 7,000 adults, shows that Britons more broadly are becoming less sexually active. In 1990, the average person between 16 to 44 in the UK had sex roughly four times a month. By 2024, that had halved to twice a month. Speaking to the Daily Mail, lead researcher of the study Soazig Clifton, honorary associate professor at University College London (UCL), described the findings as 'striking'. 'Often in surveys like this we'll see small changes over time,' she said. 'But it's relatively unusual to see big differences like this.' The scientists pointed to increasing time spent indoors and online, more youngsters living at home and the decline in alcohol drinking as potential drivers of the trend. Millennials who have never had sex also told this newspaper that the explosion of dating apps are partly to blame, while others preferred to stay celibate. The findings come from the latest National Survey of Sexual Attitudes and Lifestyles (Natsal), a study published in The Lancet that has analysed sexual behaviour trends across England, Scotland and Wales from 1990 to 2024. The once-in-a-decade study, led by researchers from UCL and other British institutions, surveyed more than 6,700 people aged 16 to 59 across the UK. The researchers also discovered rising numbers of people having same-sex encounters across Britain, alongside rising use of pornography and an increase in sexually explicit messages. The proportion of men having sex with other men tripled from 4 per cent in 2010 to 12.3 per cent in 2024. Elsewhere, the number of young women who don't wish to have children has quadrupled. One troubling finding is the dramatic drop in testing for chlamydia – the most common sexually transmitted bacteria. The proportion of sexually active 16 to 24-year-olds who were screened for the infection between 2022 and 2024 was just 18 per cent, compared to 54 per cent a decade ago. If untreated, the condition can trigger long-term problems such as pelvic inflammatory disease – an infection of the female reproductive organs that could result in infertility. The decline is likely to be partly explained by the switch from in-clinic STI screenings to online services whereby patients order a testing kit to their home, according to Professor Nigel Field, an expert in infectious disease at UCL. 'Digital services aren't great for everyone - especially those living at home with their parents who might not want to be receiving test kits in the post,' he said. The proportion of young people aged 16 to 24 who have never had sex remained remarkably stable up until the last decade. Between 1990 and 2012, around one in five men and women had never had sex. However, by 2022 to 2024, those figures had soared to 37.7 per cent for women and 28.4 per cent among men. While the researchers stressed they could not establish reasons for any of the study's findings, Prof Clifton, who is the academic director of Natsal, offered many possible explanations about the rise in virgins. Ben Harris, 33, from Kent, who appeared on season one of Channel 4 TV show Virgin Island, said apps like Tinder and Hinge were fuelling people's 'egos' 'One theory is that digital technology is playing a strong driving role,' she said. 'Tech has changed all of our lives in many different ways, especially younger generations - for instance, how we spend our time socialising with each other. 'There's a theory that this has had an impact on our sexual behaviour.' Another possible factor, she suggested, is that more young adults are living with their parents for longer. 'That might be playing a role in basic practicality, with problems meeting partners and having somewhere to have sex with a partner that's private,' she said. 'People are also going out to social events less. This would be key in where people would have met partners in the past.' The study found adults aged 25 to 34 were the most sexually active, having sex roughly three times a month on average. Overall, 77 per cent of Britons surveyed said they'd had sex in the previous year. Women reported having sex once a month, on average, rising to twice-monthly for men. Furthermore, there was a huge rise in same-sex attraction. While the number of men having sex with men trebled from 2010, the figure also more than doubled for women – from 6.8 per cent to 14 per cent in 2024. 'We can't answer why this is happening,' says Prof Clifton. 'But we do know that society has become much more accepting over the years.' Researchers also flagged that youngsters – despite not having sex – were watching a lot of pornography. Almost nine in ten men aged 16 to 24 and half of women had watched sexual content in the previous year. Almost half said they had sent or received sexually explicit images. However, Prof Clifton said people who had never had sex were actually less likely to watch pornography, meaning online activity may not be replacing physical intimacy. 'In public health and education for a long time, we have been promoting the message that people should only have sex when they feel ready,' she added. 'What we might be seeing for some individuals here is just a reflection of that conversation about sex and shift towards having better control over your sex life.'
