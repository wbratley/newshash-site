‘Not about notches on bedpost’: people in Great Britain having less sex – especially young adults

Article text

Sex among British adults has slumped in recent decades, with under-45s having half as many monthly encounters as in 1990 and one in three under-25s reporting that they have never had sexual intercourse. The fourth National Survey of Sexual Attitudes and Lifestyles, or Natsal, found the average adult under 45 now has sex twice a month, continuing a downward trend from four times a month in 1990 and three times a month in 2010. Among under-25s, the decline was dominated by a rise in the proportion of people who have never had sex, from less than a fifth of men and women in 2010 to 28% and 38% respectively, or more than a quarter of men and nearly two in five women. The survey, published across five papers in the Lancet, does not delve into the reasons for the decline, or other trends such as more same-sex intercourse, particularly among men, and a substantial drop in the number of young women wanting children. Researchers are investigating factors including less physical socialising and alcohol drinking, shifts in gender identity and sexual norms, poor support for new families, young people living with their parents for longer, poorer mental health and a surge in digital technology. The arrival of Grand Theft Auto VI might not help matters. Recent work from Rutgers University found that drinking less, living with parents, and video gaming helped to explain why young adults were having less casual sex. “Although the majority of the population are still sexually active, over time people in Britain have been having less sex, and in the past decade, that’s particularly the case for young people,” said Soazig Clifton, an honorary senior research fellow at University College London. When questioned for the survey, 77% of 16- to 59-year-olds reported having sex in the past year and 89% had had sex at least once in their lives. Prof Kaye Wellings, at the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine, co-founded Natsal but was not involved in the latest survey. She said young women today may be more able to call the shots around sex than in past decades. “Women, I think, are not prepared to just go along with either the frequency or the nature of sexual encounters just to please their partner,” she said. The average age at which young people first have sex rose to 18 in the latest survey, up from 16 in 2010. “There was a time when it was about notches on bedposts,” Wellings said, “but I just don’t think they have to prove themselves any more.” Wellings’ work on women in their 40s and 50s found many were exhausted by juggling work, home and social life, and often caring for ageing parents as well as children. For Natsal-4, about 6,700 people were randomly selected across England, Scotland and Wales, for interviews or online surveys between September 2022 and January 2024. Three previous Natsal surveys were conducted in 1990, 2000 and 2010. All trends were drawn from 16- to 44-year-olds, an age range covered by all four surveys. The Natsal-4 survey found poor sexual wellbeing was more common in younger people, those on low incomes and those who had sex for the first time after age 25 or were from sexual and gender minorities. Meanwhile, online sexual behaviour has become “part of everyday life for many”, Clifton said, with half of 16- to 24-year-olds sexting in the past year, and half of young women and 90% of young men watching pornography. The survey did not cover sexual experiences with AI chatbots. The proportion of women reporting same-sex encounters has risen steadily since 1990 and doubled in the past decade to 14%. The same period saw the first substantial rise in men having sex with men, with reports tripling from 4% to 12%. Part of the rise probably reflects more openness to reporting same-sex activity. One of the most striking results from the survey was a doubling in the proportion of women who said they definitely did not want children, rising from 8% to 17% over the past decade. The trend was dominated by women under 35, where the figure nearly quadrupled. “If it represents what women truly want, then as we’re centring it on autonomy, we should be supporting that,” said Dr Neha Pathak, co-investigator on Natsal-4 at UCL. “But we need to understand if there are other factors that are actually encouraging or driving women to make choices they don’t necessarily want.” Prof Rebecca Sear, a psychologist at Brunel University of London, believes a lack of support for childbearing is a major factor behind the rise in women deciding they do not want children. “We’re now in a system where parents are expected to raise kids alone,” she said. “We’re in a very individualistic society and that’s just too much for parents to bear. I think that partly explains why fertility has been declining and it may well explain why people are reluctant to embark on childbearing. They’re just not going to have the support they need to raise children.” Many people still risk sexually transmitted infections or unplanned pregnancies by not using effective contraception, the survey found, with 12% of women saying pregnancies were unplanned and 30% reporting mixed feelings. For the first time, the survey recorded a fall in women taking the oral pill, with a modest rise in hormonal intrauterine devices (IUDs). Vaccination for human papillomavirus (HPV) had an enormous impact, reducing rates of the cancer-causing infection by 86%. But some women born outside the UK or who belonged to particular ethnic minority groups or held strong religious beliefs were missing out. Prof Nigel Field, an infectious disease epidemiologist at UCL, said: “This is a real public health success story about preventing cancer, but services may need to adapt to better reach all women and assure equitable elimination of cervical cancer.”
