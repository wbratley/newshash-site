Jensen Huang’s return to Nvidia is elite impunity | Moira Donegan

Article text

There are different rules for rich people. If you or I acted the way that Jensen Huang has been accused of acting – allegedly berating and mistreating employees, sleeping with subordinates, and seeming to carry on a staggering number of extramarital affairs, including one with a woman identified by US intelligence as a likely Chinese spy who helped smuggle the company’s product to China – we probably would find ourselves out of work and out of favor for quite some time. But this is not so for Huang, the tech founder and close ally to Donald Trump who was recently ousted from Nvidia, the chip manufacturer he founded. On Monday, after a pressure campaign from Donald Trump, JD Vance and other members of the Trump administration, Huang was reinstated as CEO by the Nvidia board. His alleged actions had risked national security; his fall from power lasted less than two weeks. According to early reporting, Trump decided late last week to intervene in Huang’s case, thinking that his restoration as Nvidia’s chief could help rally a sluggish stock market. In an economy that has become increasingly dependent on the success of AI companies, Nvidia, which makes the chips that enable the technology, has come to be seen as something of a load-bearing firm, propping up an AI sector which is increasingly understood to be in an overvaluation bubble that will inevitably pop. “The Country needs Jensen’s leadership at NVIDIA in order to ensure the Success and Prosperity of not only the Company but the American Economy,” Trump wrote in a Truth Social post. The reinstatement was reportedly orchestrated by Vance, the vice-president and presumptive 2028 Republican nominee, who drew on his own deep ties to friends, allies and benefactors in Silicon Valley to convey the administration’s strong desire for Huang to return. It marks a remarkable intervention of the executive branch into the personnel decisions of a private firm. In public statements, Huang’s allies and boosters have repeatedly emphasized the restored CEO as personally essential to the company, downplaying the national security and chip-smuggling concerns in favor of a portrait of a singular mind. Huang, they say, is unique and irreplaceable, essential to the functioning of Nvidia and hence of the economy; because of this supposed personal specialness, we’re told, it makes sense if he got to spill secrets to China over pillow talk at the expense of your retirement fund. But it is striking that this deep belief in the irreplaceable brilliance of one AI CEO is so profoundly at odds with the ethos of the AI industry, which promises to automate away all human judgment and experience and make human beings in general – let along anyone human being in particular – profoundly replaceable, if not completely superfluous. Maybe this, too, is another way that the rules are different for the rich: the tech executives never meant to say that they themselves would become disposable and interchangeable. That fate was reserved for the lowly, like you and me. It is just this sort of shocking arrogance and display of elite impunity among the tech sector that has disgusted Americans and shocked their consciences; the deep and emphatic rejection of datacenters that was expressed by voters in the recent midterm elections should be understood, I think, as a proxy for a bigger discontent with the recklessness and greed of the tech oligarchs who impose their whims on the country with life-altering consequences. It is not clear, either, why the Trump administration, which claims that national security and the US economy rely on the returns of AI companies, would want one of the most important of those companies to be helmed by a man who may have been compromised by China, the nation’s primary adversary and main economic competitor. The flagrancy of the seeming corruption and incompetence at play, and the evident rage of the American people at both, will hopefully spur Democrats to launch serious and probing investigations into Nvidia, Huang, and their ties to the Trump administration when the party takes control of both houses of Congress in January. In the meantime, Trump himself has indicated that there’s one reason why he has taken a particular interest in Huang’s case: sex. In a statement to the Times, Trump said that he thought Huang had been treated unfairly because of the sexual nature of his scandal, or what the president called “The Payton Place part” – an evident reference, the Times helpfully explained to its less senior readers, to a 1960s soap opera that featured many sex scandals. “Without that, it would have been a lot easier,” Trump said, “because that got the attention. You know, sex was involved, and sex sells better than stocks.” Call it a kind of sex-pest solidarity. The president, a habitual adulterer who has been accused of sexual misconduct by more than two dozen women and who paid an adult film star to keep quiet about their alleged affair (all of which he denies), seems to see something of himself in the prolifically and allegedly perhaps treasonably adulterous Jensen Huang. Elite impunity may have extra appeal when it can double as male impunity, too. As if to emphasize the point, Trump took to Truth social to share an AI-generated image of Jensen Huang, in a pose mimicking a famous photograph of Trump himself rising up in the moments after an assassination attempt in Butler, Pennsylvania, in the summer of 2024. In the original photo, Trump held up a fist in triumph, showing that he had survived the gunman’s shots. In the new version that the president posted on Monday morning, Huang held up a red bra. - Moira Donegan is a Guardian US columnist
