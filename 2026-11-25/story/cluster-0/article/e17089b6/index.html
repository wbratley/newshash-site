Nuclear test veterans will get full truth, says Burnham, as he confirms independent panel

Article text

Nuclear test veterans will get full truth, says Burnham, as he confirms independent panel - Published Andy Burnham has told nuclear test veterans they will get "the full truth" as he confirmed plans for an investigation into the health legacy of Britain's Cold War-era atomic programme. The prime minister has ordered an independent panel modelled on the Hillsborough disaster investigation to assess the potential impact of testing exercises in the 1950s and 1960s. Burnham told veterans and their families that the government would treat the process with the "utmost seriousness" as he vowed to set up a "gold standard" investigation. The Ministry of Defence (MoD) has for decades denied it covered up the impact of radiation on troops ordered to carry out nuclear weapons tests. A BBC Newsnight special programme heard reaction from veterans, including John Morris, a laundry operator at a nuclear test site who went on to develop cancer and lost a four-month-old son. He believes both were linked to radiation exposure. He said: "Seventy-two years ago I was an 18-year-old, sent to Christmas Island, totally unaware of what I was being led into. "I am here today and I am looking for, and sincerely hope we will get, justice." BBC Newsnight special on nuclear test veterans Victoria Derbyshire speaks to veterans as an independent panel is announced into health impacts Watch now on iPlayer Veterans who were involved in the tests, predominantly carried out in Australia and the South Pacific, have long called for a full investigation into the long-term health effects it had on them and their children. Many of the surviving veterans say they were more likely to develop a range of illnesses in the years that followed, while their descendants say they have suffered an increased risk of hereditary illnesses and fertility problems. Veterans have recounted being ordered to fly through radioactive clouds without protection, witnessing atomic explosions with nothing to shield them from the fallout, and living on sand they believe was contaminated with nuclear material. The announcement of an independent panel is the culmination of years of campaigning by the LABRATS group and others, and comes 14 years after a legal challenge at the UK Supreme Court failed. Many veterans have died in the decades since the tests, but some of those still alive shared their stories with Newsnight on Tuesday. Brian Unthank says he witnessed three hydrogen bomb explosions in the Pacific without protective equipment and went to on develop multiple skin cancers. He said: "I've always wanted to get a prime minister... to stand up in Parliament and make a total, abject apology for the total mess-up of the way they treated us veterans and the families through this 74 years. "Next year is our 75th year of misery. Let's get it done today." Barbara Quick, whose father served in the RAF Police on Christmas Island - a major site for UK and US nuclear testing in the Pacific Ocean - told BBC Newsnight she had never been able to have children and two of her sister's children had died. She blames health complications arising from their father's exposure to radiation. She said: "If you were higher personnel you got the gas mask, you got the suit - but my father was told to turn his collar up, turn his back and cover his eyes." Steve Purse, whose father was a nuclear test veteran, was born with complex disabilities he says have never been fully diagnosed. He told BBC Newsnight the announcement was "huge" but that the panel must be allowed to provide full disclosure. He said: "No-one has listened to us like this before, it's a moment of history." Before becoming prime minister earlier this year, Burnham told BBC Newsnight the nuclear veterans had been subjected to a "criminal cover-up on an industrial scale" and backed calls for an investigation. On Tuesday, he told campaigners at Downing Street: "All I can say is you know the courage you've shown, the dignity you've shown through the whole thing, given everything that happened, is honestly something that we just are in awe of. "Your patience, your strength, your determination - and we will make sure that this process moves as quickly as possible to get you what you long deserved, which is the full truth... Obviously, I do not know what the full truth will be, but we will get it." The MoD has previously said four studies found no increased health risks for those involved with the tests, as did a study carried out by Brunel University in 2022. However, campaigners and veterans have accused the MoD of withholding full health records, including blood and urine tests, which they say would establish a link. The MoD released some official documents in July 2026, though it said some records may have been inadvertently destroyed three years earlier. The department also said the medical records that veterans and their families were trying to access would appear "complex and inconsistent" as a central database was never held by the military, in part due to the intense Cold War-era security around the nuclear tests. 'Serious allegations against the state' The independent panel announced on Tuesday will be be modelled on the Hillsborough Independent Panel and chaired by the same person, James Jones, the former Bishop of Liverpool. He told BBC Newsnight: "The nuclear veterans have for decades made serious allegations against the state about what happened to them and to their descendants through the exposure to nuclear radiation. These merit serious investigation." Downing Street said it would produce a report by October 2027 to coincide with the 75th anniversary of the first British nuclear weapons test. Defence Secretary Wes Streeting said: "It's taken too long to get here and I think today of the people who haven't lived to see this moment - and I fear for the people who might not see the outcome." He acknowledged low trust in the MoD among veterans and their families, assuring them the department would not be involved with setting up the panel or defining its remit. He said the panel would "help us to make sure that the cover-up is exposed". Asked if the government would pay compensation should the panel recommend it, Streeting said he would not pre-judge its report but that he felt "absolutely sure the findings will be uncomfortable" and the government "will have to take that on the chin".
