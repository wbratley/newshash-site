Metropolitan Police STILL 'racist, misogynistic and homophobic', report finds

Article text

Britain’s biggest police force is still racist, misogynistic and homophobic, a damning new review into the Metropolitan Police has found. The Fairfield Review into the force also warned that further scandals are also "highly likely" unless Scotland Yard is able to turn its culture around. The review, carried out by Dr Gillian Fairfield, found the Metropolitan Police failed to make "good progress" on any of their 16 targets aimed at tackling issues of culture in the force. They were set by Baroness Casey in 2023, who found the Metropolitan Police force was "institutionally racist, misogynistic and homophobic". However, this damning new report has revealed that "those fundamental, systemic issues remain" in the Metropolitan Police, and concluded "the Met still struggles to acknowledge that racism is an institutional problem." They also found many women in the force and across London are not yet confident that change is substantive and the low trust LGBT+ communities have in the Met has not improved. The review, which follows 11 months of work by Dr Fairfield, shows many cases of victims being let down by the force. In some instances, bereaved families were even convinced officers had “deliberately lied” about aspects of investigations into deaths of loved ones. One bereaved family even told the review they felt “gaslit” by officers for challenging them on how they were conducting the investigation into the murder of their loved one. Dr Fairfield found the homicide investigation team suffered from a “lack of professional curiosity, presumptuous assumptions, a lack of empathy, and inadequate supervision", which were causing harm to victims. She also found officers investigating domestic abuse were “still making unforced errors” and said “concerns remain” about how the Met is handling police perpetuated domestic abuse cases. The review did find there had been improvements made when it came to how the Met investigates rape cases. LATEST DEVELOPMENTS The Met is now being urged to redesign victim care after many victims were found to be let down by the police force. The report also found there is a lack of confidence in reporting misconduct inside the force. Many officers and staff in the Met are still scared to speak out despite the report finding that the force had improved its processes of identifying misconduct. Nearly half of all officers and staff surveyed in the force who said they felt bullied did not report it over fears of retaliation. It also found some of the behaviours and concerns in relation to custody were not confined to Charing Cross, where 15 officers were found to have committed gross misconduct. Too many officers and staff remain unconvinced that the organisation is well led, the report found. Dr Fairfield said: "A lot of work has been carried out to reform the Met since Baroness Casey's seismic report was published in 2023, and some of it is working: tougher vetting, firmer action on misconduct, and the adoption of Operation Soteria principles to rape investigations. But the evidence from my extensive engagement with Londoners and members of the Met workforce is clear: the organisation must address the remaining root causes to ensure progress does not stall or, at worst, it continues to cause harm. "The Met has largely done the more straightforward, mechanical parts of reform. The genuinely difficult work is still to come. Being uncomfortable is, I'm afraid, part of the job of reform. If the Met tries to move on too quickly – to dismiss, minimise or attempt to discredit feedback – it misses the most meaningful opportunities to improve." The Met said they accept Dr Fairfield’s findings, and Sir Mark Rowley, the Commissioner of the Metropolitan Police, said: “We have undertaken the largest integrity overhaul in British policing history, strengthened vetting and misconduct systems, removed more than 1,800 officers and staff who fell below the standards expected of them, and transformed our approach to rape investigations, leading to improved outcomes for victims. While we must continue to be relentless, this reflects real progress made through the commitment and determination of our people. "However, Dr Fairfield lays out starkly that the experience of too many victims is still not as good as we would wish it to be. She reminds us that trust in the Met among Black communities and LGBT+ communities is still trailing behind other Londoners and that the work to close those gaps and fulfil our aim of eliminating discrimination in all we do starts with listening and requires sustained commitment and structural change.” Dr Fairfield concluded the Met is more worried about how the media will react to issues in the force, rather than how to actually fix them. “In our view the only scrutiny the Met really fears is from the media,” Dr Fairfield told reporters.
