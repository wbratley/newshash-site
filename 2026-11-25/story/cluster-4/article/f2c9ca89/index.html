Metropolitan police still institutionally racist and misogynist, says report

Article text

The Metropolitan police remain institutionally racist, misogynistic and homophobic three years after a damning review into the force’s culture, a report has found. The investigation by Gillian Fairfield found “fundamental, systemic issues” and “institutional defensiveness” still existed in Britain’s biggest police force, which has struggled “to acknowledge racism as a structural problem”. The public health leader’s independent review was commissioned by Sadiq Khan, the mayor of London. Responding to the 389-page report, he called on the Met’s commissioner, Mark Rowley, to “reset his relationship with those of us tasked with holding the Met to account, and to provide Londoners with the police service they deserve”. Fairfield’s report detailed disturbing accounts from officers, including one who said women were “subject to punishment” if they reported misconduct. Another criticised the predatory behaviour of some senior officers toward younger women and recruits. Some bereaved families accused the Met of victim-blaming and gaslighting, the report said, as it detailed how forensic exhibits, statements and CCTV went missing in a homicide investigation. “It is simply not possible to change the culture of the Met if the existence of institutional racism, institutional misogyny and institutional homophobia continues to be denied, underplayed or tiptoed around,” said Khan. “I’m particularly appalled by the treatment of some of the victims highlighted in this review. Victims of crime must be treated with dignity, compassion and respect and supported to achieve the justice they deserve.” The Fairfield review examined the Met’s progress since a scathing 2023 report by Louise Casey after an officer abducted, raped and murdered Sarah Everard in 2021. The report said further scandals were “highly likely” unless the Met identified and fully addressed its culture problems. After 11 months of fieldwork, it found the Met displayed “some of the best policing and some of the worst”. It said the force had not made good progress on any of the 14 recommendations made by Lady Casey but had made some progress in nine areas and limited progress in five. Published on Wednesday, the report said: “The Casey review found the Met was institutionally racist, misogynistic and homophobic. “Our role has been to assess the Met’s progress in addressing this; we judge these fundamental, systemic issues remain.” It said some progress had been made on dealing with racism but “the Met still struggles to acknowledge racism as a structural problem rather than something people can be trained out of. As with other difficult subjects it is too often managed as a reputational threat rather than an opportunity for learning”. Defensiveness remains “deeply ingrained” and “the only scrutiny the Met really fears is from the media”. Fairfield told journalists: “The Met struggles to acknowledge racism. But if it can’t name it, it can’t face it. If it can’t face it, it can’t sort it out.” Rowley accepted the review’s recommendations but said he refused to be drawn into “what I think is a politicised debate about certain labels”. He added: “I’m not afraid of using the term racism. We have no problem with using it and confronting it. Racism, misogyny, homophobia, other forms of discrimination are something that we have to confront and have been confronting.” He said 1,800 officers and staff had been removed from the force and the Met would “keep turning over cases”. The report highlighted specialist programmes and increased recruitment and promotion for female officers, but the review team said many women in the workforce and members of the public were not confident that change was substantive. One female officer told the team: “There’s real predatory behaviour toward younger women and new recruits. They need to understand they are prey.” Trust among the LGBT community, damaged by catastrophic failings in the case of serial killer Stephen Port, has not improved, the report said, and the force was “still making false assumptions about – and sometimes blaming – LGBT+ victims”. Gay men had seen some improvement in how they were treated but lesbian and trans communities “routinely feel discriminated against”. Fairfield said the most shocking aspect of the review for her was the testimony of victims. The experiences of some victims of police-perpetrated domestic abuse evidence “an organisation perpetuating additional harm, not preventing it”. The report said bereaved families had highlighted the “lack of professional curiosity; presumptuous assumptions; lack of empathy; inadequate supervision; and wider investigative failings leading to continuing harm”. One relative told the review: “The Met repeatedly gaslit me, made me feel stupid for challenging how they conducted the (homicide) investigation.” Another said: “Even one of its own detectives told me I should lower my expectations of the Met.” The report said one family shared concerns that CCTV and doorbell footage were not seized promptly, witnesses were not interviewed and no public appeal was made. The report said: “Several families also told us, separately, that they are convinced Met officers had deliberately lied about important aspects of investigations into the death of their loved ones.” The Met had made substantial changes to misconduct handling and vetting processes but “confidence in the system remains uneven, investigations can remain lengthy, organisational learning is weak, and some officers and staff still fear retaliation for speaking up”. Only 44% of staff felt confident raising concerns about a senior officer and a quarter said they were “not so confident”. Another 17% were “not at all” confident their concerns would be acted on. One police officer with more than 20 years of experience told the review team: “Women are subject to punishment if they report misconduct.” Another said: “I have zero confidence that anything is going to happen when reporting misconduct.” The Met chose not to implement specific recommendations about its parliamentary and diplomatic protection command, where Everard’s killer, Wayne Couzens, and the rapist David Carrick served, instead bringing in its own changes. But the report found these were “yet to be fully embedded and achieve the desired impact”. It said: “High-profile abuse and challenges in custody, and royalty and specialist protection, illustrate the urgency with which the Met must identify and fully address cultures in these and other closed cultural environments. Until it does so, we judge further scandals are highly likely.” Several officers faced misconduct proceedings after a Panorama reporter went undercover in Charing Cross police station and the report said “some of these behaviours and concerns were not confined to Charing Cross”. Other problems raised included frontline policing being weakened by officers being taken off to fulfil other duties and not enough officers doing domestic abuse work. However, the team said there had been measurable progress in rape investigations. Rowley said the review recognised the “scale and pace of change” since the Casey report. “Standards are higher, performance is stronger, violence is down, criminal justice outcomes for victims of sexual violence are amongst the highest nationally, and London is safer,” he added. But he accepted that “eliminating discrimination in all we do starts with listening and requires sustained commitment and structural change”. The home secretary, Shabana Mahmood, thanked Fairfield for her review and welcomed the commissioner’s commitment to making improvements.
