Burnham puts final touches to deal that spells the start of EU surrender

Article text

Burnham puts final touches to deal that spells the start of EU surrender Andy Burnham has been accused of 'surrender' as he prepares to sign off a deal to unpick Brexit. The Prime Minister is poised to agree a 'reset' deal on Friday which will force Britain to adopt thousands of EU laws – and allow 100,000 young Europeans a year to move to the UK to work. Mr Burnham insisted the deal will deliver a modest boost to growth by slashing EU red tape on food exports to the continent. But he will agree a string of concessions in return, including permanently aligning with EU laws governing the sector and implementing a 'youth experience scheme' which could undermine efforts to tackle the UK's youth jobs crisis. European students studying at British universities could also have their tuition fees subsidised by the taxpayer at a cost of £485 million a year. Downing Street said negotiations on the final details were 'going down to the wire' ahead of talks between the PM and EU chief Ursula von der Leyen in Brussels on Friday. One insider said key elements of the deal remained in the balance on Thursday night, despite the PM giving in to EU demands to double the proposed cap on young Europeans allowed to come to the UK to 100,000 a year. Mr Burnham said he was still pushing for agreement on a new 'Channel Action Plan' to deliver greater co-operation on tackling illegal migration. Sources indicated it could eventually see the UK sign up to a new EU initiative to send failed asylum seekers to Rwanda - reviving a Tory idea that was scrapped by Labour. It's a deal: Andy Burnham and Brussels chief Ursula von der Leyen are expected to shake hands on a Brexit deal that will return Britain to the EU's orbit No 10 confirmed it will introduce new legislation into parliament on Monday that will see EU regulations on food and farming fast-tracked into British law with only minimal scrutiny. Nigel Farage dubbed the meeting in Brussels a 'surrender summit' – and warned the PM would use it as a stepping stone towards his stated ambition of rejoining the EU. The Reform UK leader told the Daily Mail the deal was a 'backwards-looking, retrograde, costly error'. He added: 'This is a huge surrender. Burnham is giving away legislative power across a very big chunk of law – we are effectively rejoining parts of the single market. 'We will end up having to borrow millions of pounds to pay into the EU's cohesion funds to build motorways in Bulgaria and Lithuania. 'And we will basically have a return to free movement for the under-30s, with 100,000 young Europeans coming to the UK for work every year. He might as well chuck Alan Milburn's report (on youth unemployment) in the bin right now. 'And for Burnham this is just the start. But if he wants to debate rejoining the EU I say bring it on.' Kemi Badenoch accused the PM of 'reopening the divisions and debates' of the Brexit referendum. The Conservative leader said she backed easier trade with the EU 'but not trade at any cost'. She said Labour should have pushed to agree mutual recognition of standards with the EU instead of signing up to accept Brussels regulations over which the UK will have no control. Mrs Badenoch said the deal would result in 'the worst of both worlds' in which the UK would become a 'rule taker' forced to pay into the EU and accept higher immigration. Downing Street accused her of defending a 'broken status quo'. The PM's press secretary told reporters there was 'a consensus in the country that change needs to happen'. He added: 'The status quo is not working. Brexit has done more harm than good.' The PM defended his proposed deal, saying: 'There's the possibility of lower food prices, and I can't turn my back on that. Lower costs for business, cutting red tape, and a really big win potentially. More collaboration on immigration, a Channel Action Plan, which is a really big deal if we can land that.' Mr Burnham has said Treasury analysis suggests the deal could add 0.4 per cent to GDP over 10 years, adding £12 billion to the economy. But Labour's former foreign secretary David Lammy described the benefits as 'small beans' and urged the PM to start negotiations on rejoining the EU. Sadiq Khan joined the call on Thursday, claiming Brexit had been 'the greatest act of economic and geopolitical self-harm in our country's history'. However, several key members of Mr Burnham's Cabinet are nervous about reopening the wider Brexit debate, including chancellor John Healey, Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood, business secretary Jonathan Reynolds and culture secretary Lisa Nandy. Mr Burnham will publish a series of 'options' for the future next month, including going back into the single market or customs union and rejoining the EU. He has said he wants to rejoin in his lifetime. But he struck a more cautious tone on Thursday night, saying he was focused on securing the current deal. Speaking after a meeting of the Coalition of the Willing in Berlin, he said it was 'not about re-running 2016. It's not about rejoining. But people, by and large, three quarters of British people support closer ties'. New polling from More in Common revealed mixed views among the public. Asked how they would vote in a referendum tomorrow, voters said they would back rejoin by 59:41. But the poll showed voters would rather the PM focused on 'making Brexit work' rather than rejoining by a margin of 46:38.
