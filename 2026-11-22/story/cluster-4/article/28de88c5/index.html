Spitting Image co-creator and puppeteer Peter Fluck who loved lampooning the great and the good dies aged 85

Article text

Spitting Image co-creator and puppeteer Peter Fluck who loved lampooning the great and the good dies aged 85 Peter Fluck, one half of the Spitting Image duo who revolutionised TV satire, has died aged 85. For more than a decade from 1984, Fluck and Roger Law had audiences falling off their chairs with laughter thanks to their cast of outrageous and irreverent latex puppets. For a while, Spitting Image was TV's most expensive light-entertainment show at £300,000 an episode, and among the most popular with Sunday night audiences peaking at 15 million viewers. Lashing out in all directions, Fluck and Law memorably skewered Margaret Thatcher as a tyrant in a shirt and tie; her eventual successor, Sir John Major, was grey from top to toe, seen mostly pushing peas around a plate; while the Queen Mother appeared as a batty old gambling addict with a Brummie accent. After a cautious beginning on ITV, there was no question of deference: even the Queen, the Duke of Edinburgh and his late Holiness John Paul II were grist to the Spitting Image humour mill. Some of the caricatures caused genuine offence. David Steel, former leader of the Liberal Party, was relentlessly targeted as the tiny sidekick of then SDP leader David Owen – he lived in Owen's pocket – and seemed diminished not just on screen but politically too. Deputy Labour Leader Roy Hattersley emitted plumes of spittle whenever he opened his mouth. Peter Fluck (left) and Roger Flaw, the caricaturists and creators of Spitting Image, with puppets of Norman Tebbit and Michael Foot A spitting image of 'Her Majesty The Queen enjoying tea and a biscuit with one of her beloved corgis while reading a newspaper Fluck himself once told The Daily Telegraph: 'I just hate people who stand up there and tell us, "I should be organising you all". I just hate them.' Fluck and Law – a former college friend, now aged 85 – were advised against pursuing the venture. The pair first came up with the idea for a show where famous politicians would be satirised by puppets in 1979. No less a figure than Jim Henson, creator of the astoundingly successful Muppet Show, told the duo their puppets would never catch on. But they persisted and in February 1984, the first episode appeared on ITV. In time it became a badge of honour to be satirised by Spitting Image. Princess Diana – herself the butt of their humour – once told Stephen Fry that it was her favourite show, 'even though all the other royals hate it'. While, according to The Times, the late Jeffrey Archer sent in tapes of his voice so they could improve their imitation. Fluck was born in Cambridge in 1941 to his father Herbert, who worked as a gardener at Magdalene College, and Ada. The satirist grew up in the countryside and as a child developed a love of ornithology, the study of birds. He attended Cambridgeshire High School for Boys and his application to Cambridge College of Art was later accepted based on several drawings he had done of house sparrows. Fluck met Anne-Cécile de Bruyne, his future wife, at the art school. They were married in 1963 and went on to have a daughter and a son. During his career, Fluck worked on magazine covers for The Economist and devised political cartoons and illustrated for Labour Weekly and The Times respectively. He then began teaching at the Colchester School of Art, in Essex, during the 1970s before forming a partnership with Spitting Image co-creator Roger Law in 1976. The pair created 3D caricature models made from clay and plasticine. It was this work that in the 1980s became the TV show Spitting Image.
