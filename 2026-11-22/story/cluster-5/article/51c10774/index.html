'I'll have this burden for ever': Devastated father JOSH FOSTER breaks his silence on the split second his son slipped from his arms and fell to his death from an open hotel window in Cyprus

'I'll have this burden for ever': Devastated father JOSH FOSTER breaks his silence on the split second his son slipped from his arms and fell to his death from an open hotel window in Cyprus By ANDREW JEHRING, CHIEF FOREIGN CORRESPONDENT Published: | Updated: Watching his young son and daughter splashing in the hotel pool, Joshua Foster remembers thinking his life was little short of wonderful. For years he and his wife had struggled to start a family; an experience that made the bond between these university sweethearts unbreakable. And now, here they were with an inquisitive little girl, aged five, and a delightful two-year-old boy, soaking up the sun on the first day of their summer holiday in Cyprus. ‘We had a girl and a boy – that’s everything we wanted,’ Josh, 37, an AI director at accountancy firm KPMG, tells me in an exclusive interview with the Daily Mail. ‘It was such a happy day. I remember doing donkey rides and playing with the kids in the pool. We were throwing little rockets in. My daughter would dive down to catch them.’ They were staying at the five-star King Evelthon hotel in Chlorakas, a coastal village north of Paphos. Cyprus was, he says, their ‘happy place’. His parents-in-law had owned a holiday home there for decades and they wanted their children to build the same precious memories their mother had growing up. That wish is now an impossibility. Hours after their arrival on July 12, Josh was waiting with his son and father-in-law for a lift on the fourth floor to go down to dinner. He momentarily lifted the boy up to a raised windowsill to see the view of the pool below. Joshua Foster, an AI director at accountancy firm KPMG, says Cyprus used to be the family's ‘happy place’ The view from the fourth floor window from which Josh's two-year-old son fell to his death The outside of the five-star King Evelthon Hotel in Chlorakas, Cyprus, where the family were staying on their holiday He believed the large paned window was shut. It wasn’t. In the blink of an eye, his son slipped from his grasp and fell 33ft to his death. Josh sprinted down the stairs to where his son lay but the toddler could not be saved. He took his last breath in his mother’s arms as she sang to him. Yet the agony was only just beginning. Within the next 24 hours, Josh was to be arrested, denied a lawyer, handcuffed and thrown into a police cell before appearing in court the following morning. All the while his wife, who faced the brutal task of identifying their son’s body alone, had to endure the added agony of acting normally around their daughter, who was yet to learn that her brother had died. Detained for eight days – the maximum time possible – Josh was charged with ‘causing death by a rash, thoughtless, or dangerous act’ and prevented from leaving the country. The ban was temporarily lifted for a month in August to allow him to bury his son. As if he hadn’t suffered enough, Josh had to return to Cyprus in September to face charges. He finally walked free last month, after pleading guilty, with the judge saying that he would ‘feel guilt for the rest of his life’. Today, back in the UK and with his loving wife at his side, Josh has bravely chosen to share the harrowing details of what happened – not to elicit sympathy but to try to ensure no other family endures the torture they experienced. ‘I have a burden,’ he says through tears. ‘I’ll have that my whole life. But we want to make sure this doesn’t happen again.’ The family has fiercely fought to protect their son’s identity over these traumatic past three months, not releasing a picture and asking for him not to be identified. Jaxon was only named last week when an inquest opened into his death in Essex. The Daily Mail names him briefly again here, with his parents’ permission, only because it became a matter of public record against their wishes. Meanwhile, his mother, a 38-year-old physiotherapist, asks that she is not identified as she continues to process what has happened. For as Josh tells the Daily Mail in an interview for which he has received no payment, their grief remains impossibly raw. ‘Dishing up the dinners... to begin with I’d get his bowl out and then you just realise...’ says Josh, who holds his wife’s hand firmly throughout our interview. ‘Our dog started barking the other night and I said, “Shh, you’ll wake the children up.” Because you forget.’ They have each had to navigate difficult conversations with their daughter, who understands that her brother is ‘in heaven’. ‘It’s very difficult,’ says Josh. ‘She waves at the sky and says, “I love you” to him and blows a kiss. ‘She’s lost her best friend. She’s gone from having a sibling to being an only child.’ While Josh struggles to speak without being overwhelmed by emotion, his wife says she is still ‘numb’. Recalling the lead up to the holiday which was to be spent with Mrs Foster’s parents – and just weeks after Josh and his wife had celebrated their tenth wedding anniversary – Josh says that no one was more thrilled to get on the plane than their son. ‘He was super excited,’ he says of the laidback toddler with a cheeky smile. ‘He was such a perfect little boy.’ It was also the first year the family were to stay on the island in a hotel, as Mrs Foster’s parents had sold their apartment there in 2024. On arrival, alert to any potential danger, his wife had placed a chair on either side of the patio doors leading to the balcony so she would be woken if any child tried to go out in the night. Around 5pm on the first evening, the family decided on an early dinner. The boys headed down ahead of the girls, with the toddler and his grandfather reaching the lift first. It stopped on their floor and, as the doors creaked open, the excited toddler walked in before stepping back outside to wait for his dad, who had yet to appear. The lift door shut and descended without them. ‘It’s one of those “what if” moments,’ Josh says now. ‘What if he had gone down... or I’d come out earlier...? You can go on like that for ever.’ Josh takes a deep breath before describing what happened next. ‘It’s very traumatic,’ he says. ‘My mind doesn’t want to go there.’ The lift, he says, took several minutes to arrive, so his son asked to see the pool he had been playing in through a raised window beside it. The window, which measures 9ft by 4ft, slides open horizontally with overlapping panes, meaning it is hard to tell at first glance if it’s open or shut. Josh says he was certain the pane was closed – after all, he reasoned, how could it not be from the height of the fourth floor? He lifted his son on to the sill. Then, in a split second, Josh’s stomach dropped and his heart exploded. ‘I just remember the despair of him falling and watching him fall,’ he says, barely able to get the words out. ‘There was just the feeling of... it was just such a shock that the window... when the glass wasn’t there. I put him up thinking he was going against a glass pane... so then the weightlessness. And then he wasn’t there.’ Josh screamed out in horror, then raced to the veranda 33ft below. ‘There was just a laser focus getting to him. I picked him up. I held him and screamed.’ Moments earlier, hearing her husband wailing, Josh’s wife told her daughter to stay in the room with her grandfather and dashed outside. ‘I knew instantly my life had changed,’ she says. She ran down and found Josh with their son. Taking him in her arms, she asked staff and guests surrounding her to leave them alone as she sang him a lullaby. ‘I wanted that moment with my son,’ she says. Paramedics arrived minutes later and the child was taken to hospital but could not be saved. They had just over an hour with their son in a room. ‘We cuddled and kissed him,’ Josh says. ‘We said goodbye.’ In the corridor outside were non-uniformed police officers who said Josh needed to go to the station. ‘We won’t have him for long,’ they assured his wife. At the station, a police officer told the distressed father: ‘The more trust you place in me, the easier this is going to be.’ Josh recounted that evening’s events as best he could – but even at this early stage there were worrying signs. ‘They write your statement for you. They draft it in Greek and then translate it into English. ‘He was saying things like, “So you were swinging him, yeah?” But I didn’t swing him so I tried to insist that he change that word. ‘You’re so vulnerable. I really put my faith in him when I should have held my ground. At 10.30pm they told me they were going to place me under arrest. I immediately asked for a lawyer. They told me, “Why do you need a lawyer? Don’t you trust us?”’ At 3am, Josh was taken to Paphos police station, thrown into a cell with another inmate – barefoot after losing his sandals – and left to sleep on the floor. At 9 o’clock the next morning, he was taken to Paphos District Court, wearing a borrowed shirt, trousers and shoes from his cell mate. He had no legal representative and no family member – his wife hadn’t even been told he was going to court. Unknown to him, the statement suggesting he had been ‘swinging’ his son made its way to local reporters. Meanwhile, back at the hotel, his wife had been waiting sleeplessly for news. At 7am, she’d taken a call from police telling her to get to the capital, Nicosia, a 90-mile journey, by midday to identify her son. Asked if they could send a car for the two-and-a-half hour journey, she was told to ‘get a bus or a taxi’. Nobody told her that her husband had appeared in court and been remanded in custody. Later that day, Mrs Foster received a call from the British High Commission who said they had spoken to the police and reiterated that Josh ‘doesn’t need a lawyer’. After this, she returned to the hotel, quickly realising she would need to change accommodation. Neither the management of the hotel nor Jet2holidays, the package firm they’d used, initially saw fit to move Mrs Foster from their room which was just yards from the window. When asked, Jet2holidays relocated her to a crowded hotel in the centre of town. After three days, she moved to somewhere more private. By now she knew that Josh was in prison and, along with his parents who had flown in to support them, went to visit him. Despite pleading for Josh to be allowed out to tell his daughter that her little brother had died, the authorities refused. The couple had no choice but to arrange a FaceTime call with Josh in prison to allow them to explain to their little girl. Understandably, they want the details of this heartwrenching conversation to remain private. Afterwards, Josh’s wife took their daughter to the harbour and bought her a Hello Kitty balloon. She let go of it by accident. ‘Don’t worry, Mummy,’ she said. ‘That’s going up to my brother in heaven.’ On July 21, the police formally charged Josh with ‘causing death through a rash, thoughtless or dangerous act’. Josh says that his daughter ‘waves at the sky and says, “I love you”’ to her brother', adding that she has ‘lost her best friend’ and ‘gone from having a sibling to being an only child’ Josh was arrested, denied a lawyer, handcuffed and thrown into a police cell before having to appear in court the morning after his son's death When Josh arrived at Paphos District Court, he was wearing a borrowed shirt, trousers and shoes from his cell mate The offence carries a maximum sentence of four years imprisonment. He was bailed after his family put up €30,000 (£25,000) and joined his family in a nearby villa. Josh tells me he was nervous about seeing his father-in-law again, the only other witness to the tragedy. ‘But he just hugged me,’ Josh says. ‘He understood what an awful accident it was.’ Heartbreakingly, Josh was also worried about seeing his daughter. ‘She gave me the biggest hug.’ After a week his wife made the agonising decision to go home with their daughter to prepare for their son’s funeral. Back in Cyprus, Josh had hired a barrister, Alexandros Alexandrou, who was horrified at his treatment. On August 5, the condition barring Josh from leaving the island was lifted so he could attend his son’s funeral. He and his wife focused on giving their son ‘a perfect send-off’. They wrote a song for him, released doves and attendees wore blue, his favourite colour. ‘We tried to do it as a celebration of our son’s life rather than a sad thing,’ Josh says. Days later, it was time for their daughter to go back to school and start Year One. On September 14, Josh returned to Cyprus with his parents. Two days later, on what would have been his son’s third birthday, an appeal to drop the case was rejected by the Attorney General. Josh knew his wife and in-laws would be called as witnesses and forced to look at images from the tragedy if he fought the case. Crucially, too, the particulars of the charge specified that the act did not amount to culpable negligence and was without intention. In the end, Josh says it was a simple decision to plead guilty. On October 16, Judge Froso Christodoulidou let him walk free with a €3,000 (£2,500) fine. Asked if it’s difficult living with his conviction, Josh looks almost perplexed. ‘No,’ he says faintly. ‘So much else bothered me more. My son’s died and I wasn’t able to be with my daughter and wife.’ Josh, who plans to return to work when he is able, has reported himself to the Institute of Chartered Accountants. They must determine if the conviction brings discredit on him or the profession which could see him struck off. He awaits the outcome. Today, the couple are focused on their daughter, who helped dig a memorial garden at their home for her brother. While they feel their way through grief, it’s clear Josh and his wife desperately want those who dealt with their tragedy to understand the many errors inflicted on them. Why was Josh advised that he didn’t need a lawyer? Why did it take two weeks for his wife to be given psychological support? And what questions were asked of the hotel, where the Daily Mail has revealed three British tourists have now died in four years? The Foreign Office said it ‘supported’ Josh, while the Attorney General’s office said it had received no complaint. The police say the case is being ‘carefully reviewed’, while Jet2holidays said they provided ‘total support’ including moving the family. Both the travel firm and King Evelthon insisted the windows ‘comply with the applicable safety regulations’. A hotel spokesman said management ‘personally conveyed their condolences’. But for Josh, it’s unforgivable that the hotel has not put up a warning sign alerting guests to potential danger. The Daily Mail previously published images showing the window remained open weeks after the tragedy. ‘The most angry I feel about the whole thing is that window’s still open,’ says Josh. ‘Because I want to make sure that nothing like this ever happens again.’
