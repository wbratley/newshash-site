Andy Burnham’s EU deal leaves Brexit open to question | Letters

Article text

Your article (Burnham seals trade and youth mobility deals in biggest step towards EU since Brexit, 20 November) marks a welcome moment of political realism after years of denial about the consequences of Brexit. I wholeheartedly agree with Andy Burnham when he said: “This is a good day for Britain and this is a good day for Europe.” For years, the evidence has pointed in the same direction, that the UK economy is worse off as a result of Brexit. Trade barriers have increased, growth has suffered and businesses have been forced to navigate unnecessary bureaucracy. Many of the industries that Brexit supporters claimed would flourish have instead struggled. Fishing, farming, construction and touring musicians have all faced new obstacles, costs and restrictions. Meanwhile, younger generations have seen opportunities to study, work, travel and collaborate across Europe reduced through no fault of their own. The benefits of closer trade links with the UK’s nearest and largest market cannot be overestimated. Yet Nigel Farage and Conservative Brexit supporters have failed to deliver the economic opportunities and global trade advantages they promised. Andy Burnham deserves credit for acknowledging that Brexit has failed in many respects and for supporting practical steps to rebuild relations with the EU. The choice is no longer between leave and remain; it is between decline and renewal. The country deserves leaders willing to put economic reality ahead of ideology, and this agreement is a welcome step in that direction. Stuart Finegan Lewes, East Sussex The sight and sound of former prime ministers criticising Andy Burnham’s proposal to open a discussion on Britain’s future relationship with the EU is unedifying. It is as though they have learned nothing and forgotten nothing, not least their own responsibility for the toxic mess of Brexit. In advising Andy Burnham to “move on”, Theresa May appears to overlook her own stasis (Report, 22 November). The point of democracy is surely the right of the people to change their minds. We accept this during the routine electoral cycle. Why can we not do so regarding our relationship with the EU? It is inconsistent to argue that the 2016 referendum was binding for all time whereas the 1975 EEC referendum was not. Labour’s next manifesto should therefore include a commitment to explore and openly present the options, followed by discussions with the EU on the terms that might be on offer and by a referendum, conducted on the same basis as that of 2016, whether to accept those terms. Julian Roberts Leatherhead, Surrey Your report describes Andy Burnham’s push for closer European alignment as a political gamble (Misty-eyed Burnham gambles on closer ties with EU as he throws down gauntlet to Tories and Reform, 20 November. Yet by anchoring his case in the prospect of stronger GDP growth, he is simply returning to the hard-headed economic logic that has governed Britain’s relationship with Europe for more than half a century. When Britain joined the European Economic Community in 1973, the argument was never built on federalist sentiment. It sought access to a dynamic continental market to revive sluggish growth and boost exports. Two years later, in the 1975 referendum, voters overwhelmingly backed remaining for the exact same bread-and-butter reasons, just as British economic self-interest later drove the creation of the single market. Seen through this historical lens, 2016 was not the culmination of a longstanding economic critique but a political aberration. In the runup to the Brexit referendum, rational debate over trade and living standards was hijacked by a toxic combination of Boris Johnson’s ruthless personal ambition for the Tory leadership and visceral anxieties over borders – anxieties turbocharged by Angela Merkel’s 2015 decision to open Germany’s doors to about a million Syrian refugees. Leave campaigners weaponised those images. Britain’s European story has always been one of economic necessity; Burnham is merely picking up where we left off. Lindsay Bray Ferndown, Dorset Among all the scenarios citing the fact that things are different now than in 2016, the most significant is not being commented on. The current 18- to 28-year-old demographic was not eligible to vote. Perhaps an opinion poll of this cohort of now eligible potential voters would tell us a lot about the direction of travel. Helen Elliott Ruishton, Somerset Ten years ago, the UK had a referendum to decide whether to stay or leave the EU. In a once-in-a-generation vote, this country decided to leave. As a generation is generally accepted as being between 25 and 30 years, why is there now talk of rejoining? A democratic decision was made and must be respected. We voted out: out means we leave, not hover on the doorstep. Nick Smith London
