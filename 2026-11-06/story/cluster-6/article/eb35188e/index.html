Gavin Newsom spares every murderer on California's death row in one of his final acts as Governor

Article text

Gavin Newsom spares every murderer on California's death row in one of his final acts as Governor Gavin Newsom has spared every murderer on California's death row in one of his final acts as governor. Newsom on Thursday announced that he was commuting the sentences of all 548 prisoners on death row in the state. He has reduced every sentence to life in prison without parole, he revealed in an open letter to California residents. The Democrat said his decision comes after 'nearly three decades of openly opposing' capital punishment, which he finds morally objectionable. Newsom acknowledged that the prisoners on death row caused 'indescribable suffering' to their victims and their loved ones, but went on to argue that he has 'concluded the state cannot morally kill them.' 'State-sanctioned killing is incompatible with California's values, undermines the foundations of our legal system and diminishes each of us,' he wrote in the letter. 'I know we cannot execute people in our imperfect legal system and remain true to our core values, first among them justice and human decency.' Although Newsom has announced his mass clemency plan, it is unclear if he is actually permitted to reduce every prisoner's sentence under California law. Gavin Newsom, pictured, has spared every murderer on California's death row in one of his final acts as governor Newsom has commuted the sentences of all 548 prisoners on death row in the state, reducing every sentence to life in prison without parole According to the state constitution, the governor can unilaterally grant clemency and commute sentences for inmates who have only been convicted of a single felony. But the California Supreme Court's approval is required to grant clemency to anyone convicted of a felony more than once. This means the court would have to approve 382 of the commutations announced Thursday, or roughly 70 percent, according to numbers provided by Newsom's office. Newsom said the decision was among the most difficult choices of his career because it will surface painful memories for murder victims' families. 'As Governor, I alone bear responsibility for this decision and its impact, just as I alone would bear responsibility for each execution,' he wrote. LA County District Attorney Nathan J. Hochman pushed back against Newsom's decision. In a statement, he said that while the California governor has the 'constitutional power' to commute existing death sentences, 'that power does not extend to eliminating the death penalty.' Hochman added: 'Only the Legislature or the voters can do that. Californians have been asked on two separate ballot initiatives to abolish the death penalty and both times they have chosen to keep it for the most serious crimes. 'Until the law is changed, my office will continue to evaluate every death penalty-eligible case with the rigor, care and restraint these decisions demand. As always, we will be guided by the facts, the law and the evidence. If the Legislature or the voters remove the death penalty, we will follow that law just as faithfully. Newsom had been weighing the clemency move for months, but increased talks with his advisors after the botched execution of Christa Pike in Tennessee on September 30 'My thoughts today are with the families of victims, who deserve a justice system that honors the law as it is written and pursues it with fairness and due process.' Los Angeles County Deputy DA Jonathan Hatami, who was the lead prosecutor of the high-profile torture and murder case of eight-year-old Gabriel Fernandez in Palmdale, also hit out at the decision. Gabriel's parents, Isauro Aguirre and Pearl Fernandez were convicted of starving and torturing the young boy and causing his death after he suffered a severe beating on May 22, 2013 that left him brain dead. He then passed away two days later. Gabriel's brother and sister, who were eight and 10 years old at the time of the trial, testified against their parents. The case made national news and was featured in a 2020 Netflix documentary The Trials of Gabriel Fernandez. Hatami said he spoke to Gabriel's siblings, who are now young adults, and told them about Newsom’s decision to commute the sentences. He said the family is upset because they never received a call from the governor’s office concerning this latest decision. ‘Gabriel was a little boy who was tortured for eight months and the jury heard all of the testimony, including from his brother and sister, who were also so young at the time. The jury decided on capital punishment and the judge affirmed that, and that was the one closure that the family got,' the deputy DA said. 'For one person to just violate the will of the jurors and judge to make a unilateral decision like this feels like a violation.’ 'I understand people's position on capital punishment. So I get it, and I get those positions. But somebody unilaterally making that decision is not how America is supposed to be run.' ‘It's mostly really disappointing that that one person made this a political decision to violate the will of the voters. It's a pretty sad situation. 'If you want to go a case-by-case basis, that is fine. That's how the process should work, but not like this,' he added. If his mass clemency moves forward, some of California's most notorious killers could be spared. Among those is serial killer Charles Ng who was sentenced to death in 1999. He raped, tortured and murdered between 11 and 25 people in Calaveras County between 1983 and 1985. Another serial killer, Wayne Adam Ford, is among those currently on death row. The former long-haul trucker and ex-US Marine, 64, abducted, strangled, killed and dismembered four women – Tina Renee Gibbs, Patricia Ann Tamez, Lanett Deyon White and Kerry Ann Cummings – between 1997 and 1998. The notorious Yosemite Park Killer, Cary Stayner, was sentenced to death after he was convicted of murdering four women near the national park in 1999. He too could be spared by Newsom's order, despite the California Supreme Court having denied his death penalty appeal earlier this year. Vincent Brothers, a former elementary school vice principal, was sentenced to death in 2007 for the murders of his wife, three children and mother-in-law in July 2003. Brothers shot dead his wife Joanie, 39, and their children, Marques, four, Lyndsey, two, and Marshall, six weeks, inside their Bakersfield home. He also killed Joanie's 70-year-old mother, Earnestine Harper. The crime scene was staged to look like a botched burglary and Brothers attempted to establish an alibi by flying to Ohio before the murders. He then rented a car, drove to California and returned to Ohio afterwards. DeLeon, a former child actor, started transitioning while behind bars after she was sentenced to death in 2009 for the murders of Thomas and Jackie Hawks. DeLeon, who was born John Jacobson Jr, overpowered the couple on their 55ft yacht and tied them to the boat's anchor before throwing them overboard in 2004. During her trial, lawyers said that she did so to pay for a sex change – having forced the couple to sign over their assets before they were killed. Her wishes came true when the California taxpayer started footing the bill for her surgery in April 2023. All executions in California take place at the infamous San Quentin State Prison Newsom's clemency ruling comes as he prepares to leave office after eight years. In 2019, he placed a moratorium on executions in the state, but hundreds of people remained on death row. California voters rejected a ballot measure in 2016 that would have repealed the death penalty in the state. Newsom has said in the past that he feels it is wrong for the government to kill its citizens and that capital punishment in practice has discriminated against mentally ill people and people of color. The American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU), who has argued the implementation of the death penalty reflects racial bias, applauded the announcement. 'These commutations reflect what justice demands: An end to a racist, error-prone, and inhumane punishment that has burdened California for far too long,' said executive director Anthony Romero in a statement. The ACLU is involved in an ongoing legal challenge to California's death penalty statute, alleging it is racially discriminatory and unconstitutional.
