Five things we learned from Prince Harry’s interview in Ukraine

Article text

Prince Harry says he would “be there to serve my country” if he is asked to rejoin the military, in his first major interview since returning to the UK. The Duke of Sussex spoke to The Telegraph in a 40-minute interview during his surprise visit to Lviv in Ukraine on the weekend. Harry spent two days at events for Ukraine war veterans. There was a media blackout during the visit, for security reasons, until after he had left. During the interview Harry spoke frankly about his “deep connection” with the people of Ukraine, about Russia’s “appalling” attacks on civilian infrastructure and his sense that the “whole of Europe” has been at war with Russia for the last decade. Despite saying he wanted to avoid talking about politics, he said he hoped US attitudes to the war would change when the post-midterms Congress begins in January. Here are some of the highlights of the interview. ‘I will 100 per cent be there’ As the UK holds conversations about national resilience, and preparation for war, Harry was asked whether he believes Britain has the resolve to face up to enemies. “If asked to sign up again, if the need is there, then I will 100 per cent be there to serve my country alongside my fellow veterans and service personnel,” Harry said. “The whole of Europe has now woken up to the threat [from Russia]. I think the threat’s been there and been consistently there for the last 15 years. Just because there’s not drones, bullets, and rockets flying at us, that doesn’t mean that we’re not at war. “I feel like we’ve been at war for the last decade… those of us that have served should be ready to serve again.” Harry served in the military for 10 years, including two front line tours of Afghanistan before he left the army in 2015. In 2021 the late Queen stripped him of his honourary military titles. Harry said he saw his continuing role to be to do “whatever I can do to help from a defence standpoint and the national resilience…  learning from past mistakes, and being able to build enough of that resilience so that we can defend and protect ourselves”. However he acknowledged it was “not up to me” whether he returned to the military. In the meantime, he said to “watch this space” for more work with veterans, specifically in helping them transition to civilian life. Asked if he’d taken the government advice to stockpile, he said it was a “fantastic question”. “I think we should all be nervous, and I think we should be prepared.” ‘A sign of desperation’ Harry said he was worried about the effects of Russian influence on Europe, saying “my biggest concern is Europe or parts of Europe turning on itself, because I think strategically, that is exactly what they want”. On the prospect of peace negotiations between Russia and Ukraine, Harry said it was “a very highly political question which I’m not going to get involved with”. However, he said Russia needed an “exit strategy” from the war. The Duke of Sussex visited the Superhumans Center for war veterans in Lviv, Ukraine (Photo by Efrem Lukatsky / Press Association) “The amount of soldiers, Russians, that these [Ukrainian] guys are killing on the front line is insane, so there is no version of where Russia is making any more advances,” he said. It’s quite the opposite.” Harry said rocket and drone strikes by Russia on civilian infrastructure were “a sign of desperation, a sign that they are losing”. “It’s appalling that civilian infrastructure, especially hospitals and medical centres are being targeted,” he said. “Humanitarian aid centres are being targeted as well. International law must mean something. “So I think the rest of the world could and should do more in that regard.” ‘There’s been a change’ Specifically asked about United States’ support for Ukraine, and whether the Republican rout in the midterms might improve support, Harry initially laughed and said “I’m not going to answer”. However, he went on, “I believe there’s been a big separation between what American society feel regarding support for Ukraine, which I think is incredibly high and always has been, compared to a certain group or, in this case, an administration that was perhaps thinking about it differently. “As of January, things are going to change, so I think we should all be looking forward to that.” But he said the six years he had spent in America had shown him “there are key people and key platforms that could and should be doing better”. “What they’re doing at the moment, I think, is stoking a fire, which is incredibly dangerous.” In what he said was a reference to “social media platforms and stuff like that”, he said “some of these very powerful players across across the world and specifically in America should be playing more of a leadership role to contain the chaos.” ‘Bad actors out there’ Harry said he wanted to make sure the “stories are heard” from Ukraine, “otherwise the narrative out there can easily change”. Harry said veterans he had spoken to in Ukraine were worried they were losing support in Europe and around the world, as they watched misinformation spread online. “I think that what [one veteran] was seeing on social media was propaganda,” he said. “I don’t know whether it’s from Russia, I don’t know whether it’s coming from America, wherever it is … it’s very easy for individuals or groups to be consumed by that.” “There is very little that we can do about it because of the way that social media is right now… there will always be bad actors out there trying to spin a different narrative.” He said much of the war at the moment was psychological. “It’s very easy for propaganda and social media and the Russians to try to divide a country. That certainly seems to be one of the strategic aims at the moment, is to try to literally destroy the head of the country, being in Kyiv. “But what I see is a country united.” ‘It keeps pulling me back’ Harry was asked why he has committed so much of his time to the Ukraine cause, and he said it was because of Ukraine’s close connection to the “spirit” of his Invictus charity for wounded military veterans. “It keeps pulling me back,” he said. A year and a half ago he first went to Superhumans Centre in Lviv, a specialised war trauma and rehabilitation clinic that provides prosthetics, reconstructive surgery, rehab and psychological care to former soldiers. “It’s hard to go to a place like that and not feel a deep connection with the people that you’ve met,” Harry said. “The spirit of the wounded here in Ukraine is very [strong]… and the Invictus spirit lives in every single Ukrainian person, not just veterans, but [every] person that I’d met, the resilience of the individuals, the resilience of this country is exceptional.” There are now 27 nations in Invictus, but Harry said it was “understandable” that so much of Invictus’ effort focused on a country at war, with three million veterans and well over 100,000 critically injured. Harry was last in Ukraine in April to support the Halo Trust, a Scotland-based charity that clears landmines and other explosive remnants of war.
