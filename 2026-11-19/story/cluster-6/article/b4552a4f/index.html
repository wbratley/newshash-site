Bayesian crew unaware of safety risk before Sicily sinking killed seven including Mike Lynch

Article text

Bayesian crew unaware of safety risk before Sicily sinking killed seven including Mike Lynch The crew of the Bayesian superyacht were unaware of a critical safety risk which left the vessel vulnerable to powerful winds before it sank off Sicily in 2024, killing Mike Lynch and six others The crew aboard the Bayesian superyacht were unaware of a critical safety risk which left the vessel dangerously exposed to powerful winds before it plunged beneath the waves off Sicily, killing seven people, British investigators have revealed. British tech mogul Mike Lynch, 59, and his daughter Hannah, 18, were amongst the seven victims, who perished when the 56-metre (184ft) Bayesian sank off the Sicilian coast on August 19, 2024. The definitive report into the disaster from the Marine Accident Investigation Branch (MAIB), released today, appears to contradict recent findings from Italy, where prosecution experts have reportedly attributed the tragedy to crew errors rather than any faults with the yacht itself. Charlotte Golunski and James Emslie, who managed to escape the stricken vessel with their young daughter, have stated in High Court filings the catastrophe was the result of serious safety failings relating to both the yacht's design and the conduct of its crew. The family are currently fighting an attempt by two companies linked to the yacht to cap the level of compensation they could be compelled to pay out. 'The crew were reacting to the situation they found themselves in' They claim passengers were left to "scramble to safety on their own with very limited assistance from the captain and crew who had ultimate responsibility for their safety". Matthew Chapman KC, representing the family, said: "In the circumstances, they knew or are likely to have known that the continued operation of the yacht, including in the prevailing weather conditions, exposed passengers to a probability of death or personal injury and, notwithstanding such knowledge, they permitted the yacht to be operated, in weather of the kind that was forecast and experienced at the time that the yacht was lost." The barrister argued that the companies acted recklessly as they had "actual knowledge that defects in the design, construction and operation of the yacht would probably result in the loss of life, the suffering of personal injury and the consequential losses and expenses". The MAIB launched an investigation as the Bayesian was UK-registered. Simon Graves, the MAIB's principal inspector, said: "The crew were reacting to the situation they found themselves in. The weather forecasts for that day gave generally reasonable weather with the possibility of thunderstorms, but none of the weather forecasts described the weather that they actually experienced." He added: "Unfortunately, they were overtaken by events and the speed of the knockdown. They went from being in a calm situation to a violent storm to being knocked over in a very short space of time, which gave them very little time to react. Once the vessel had been knocked down, they set off the emergency rescue beacon, they released the life raft. They managed to get some of the guests out of the vessel into the life raft and gave them the best chance of survival." 'Investigation reveals unassessed design flaw left Bayesian vulnerable' Mr Graves said the British and Italian investigations had distinct aims and could quite reasonably arrive at differing conclusions. He said: "The two investigations are entirely separate, and it's quite reasonable that they will come to different conclusions because their objectives are different." The MAIB's final report concluded that the yacht was struck by winds of approximately 80mph, causing it to capsize in under 15 seconds. It was forced beyond the angle from which it could right itself and sank just 18 minutes later. Investigators established that the yacht's susceptibility to powerful winds had never been evaluated for the conditions in which it was anchored, with its sails down and its retractable centreboard raised. The centreboard is a large underwater structure that helps keep a sailing vessel stable. Because the risk had never been evaluated, the crew were unaware of how readily the yacht could be capsized in such conditions, the report stated. Mr Graves explained: "Bayesian had a vulnerability and from the design stage all the way through, there was no requirement to assess this vulnerability. So this vulnerability had not been identified." A brief timeline of the tragedy The Bayesian was anchored off the Sicilian fishing village of Porticello on the night of August 18, 2024, carrying 12 guests and 10 crew members on board. The captain had instructed those on watch to wake him should winds exceed 20 knots, roughly 23mph, or if the anchor started to drag. At 3am, winds remained calm, though lightning and storm clouds appeared to be drawing ever closer. At 3.44am, a crew member on watch uploaded a video of the encroaching storm to social media. Just 11 minutes later, winds had picked up to approximately 35mph. The captain was roused at around 4am, with the crew beginning preparations on the engines in order to move the vessel. But at 4.06am, the wind suddenly rocketed to around 80mph. The yacht was hurled onto its side, sending people and furniture crashing across the decks. Downburst caught Bayesian crew off guard Investigators believe the ferocious weather was most likely the result of a downburst - a powerful blast of air from a thunderstorm that strikes the sea and fans out with tremendous force. David Robertson, the lead investigator of the sinking, said the crew had not been adequately forewarned about this danger. He told a press briefing on Wednesday: "The forecast didn't mention the chance of violent winds, crew training hadn't covered it, and guidance didn't cover it. All of that combined to limit the crew's ability to recognise the developing hazard and take timely action." Investigators concluded that flooding was not responsible for the initial capsize. Water only became a significant issue once the yacht was already lying on its side, the report stated. The report also laid bare the desperate struggle to escape as the vessel began to sink. Five people sustained injuries "either by falling or from things falling on them", while the deck hand was "thrown into the sea", according to the report. Two guests used furniture drawers "as an improvised ladder" to escape their cabin. The skipper instructed guests and crew on an area of the deck to "swim clear of the mast and boom as the vessel was sinking". Survivors subsequently made their way onto a life raft released from the Bayesian. They were then rescued by a small boat dispatched by the yacht Sir Robert Baden Powell, which was also anchored close by. However, none of the Bayesian's life rafts automatically floated free and inflated during the sinking, the report concluded. Crew members were forced to release a raft manually. MAIB calls for sweeping safety changes The captain and a fellow crew member also swam back towards the stricken yacht in a bid to release another raft, though their efforts proved unsuccessful. Mr Graves paid tribute to the actions of the crew following the yacht's capsizing. He said: "The safety of the people who were able to leave the vessel at that point relied heavily on the training experience and not least the initiative of the crew members who were on board." The investigation was conducted alongside a criminal inquiry in Italy, where captain James Cutfield, chief engineer Tim Parker Eaton and deckhand Matthew Griffith face potential charges of multiple manslaughter and culpable shipwreck. British investigators revealed that Italian prosecutors had restricted access to certain witnesses and evidence. The report states: "It is possible that the circumstances depicted in this report may alter following disclosure of evidence withheld by the Italian authorities." Despite this, Mr Graves confirmed he was fully satisfied with the British investigation. He said: "We are content with our investigation, the investigation that we've undertaken, and the information we've used to feed into it." He went on to clarify that the MAIB's role was to prevent future accidents rather than to apportion criminal responsibility. The report additionally concluded that alcohol consumption had played no part in the sinking. Rob Loder, chief inspector of Marine Accidents said: "Our investigation found that Bayesian had a vulnerability to high winds that was not recognised, because the yacht had never been assessed at anchor with its sails down. The extreme winds that night were also not explicitly forecast, so the crew had no warning of the risk. "MAIB has published a safety flyer for the industry and a report which makes recommendations covering vessel design standards, crew training and how rare but severe weather is represented in forecasts. "I urge everyone with a part to play, including regulators, training providers and weather services, to read our report and act on it, so that a tragedy like this is not repeated." UK inquest proceedings are examining the deaths of Mr Lynch and his daughter, alongside Morgan Stanley International bank chairman Jonathan Bloomer, 70, and his wife Judy Bloomer, 71, all of whom held British nationality. Fifteen survivors, among them Mr Lynch's wife Angela Bacares, were pulled to safety. Mr Lynch and his daughter are understood to have resided in the London area, while the Bloomers were based in Sevenoaks, Kent. The Maritime and Coastguard Agency has also launched its own investigation into the vessel's sinking.
