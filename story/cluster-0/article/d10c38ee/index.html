TOBIAS ELLWOOD: I lied to MPs about our nuclear test veterans - now I know they were victims of a scandal as big as the Post Office

Article text

TOBIAS ELLWOOD: I lied to MPs about our nuclear test veterans – now I know they were victims of a scandal as big as the Post Office Eight years ago, I told the House of Commons what I now know to be an outright lie. It concerned the scandal of the 20,000 servicemen forced to witness the testing of British nuclear weapons in the Pacific and Australian desert during the 1950s and 60s. Then a defence minister with responsibility for veterans affairs, I was being challenged about whether blood samples had been taken from these men at the time, as many later claimed. These would be crucial in supporting their demand for compensation for the cancers and other terrible illnesses they went on to suffer. Like minister after minister before me, I trusted what I was told by the Ministry of Defence, which claimed it was ‘unable to locate any information’ suggesting such samples had been taken. That was what I declared to the Commons, but it was very far from the truth. Shamed In 2024, after years of inaction, the MoD was finally shamed into conducting an investigation that revealed medical examinations and blood testing were carried out for most of that period, across all three services. It also confirmed that some blood-testing records were missing and that others had been destroyed through administrative error. Did this amount to deliberate deception by the MoD? That is one of the questions I hope will be answered by the Hillsborough-style independent inquiry expected to be announced by Andy Burnham this week. The MoD said it had found no evidence of intentional concealment. But I believe there may have been a cover-up on a scale comparable to, if not bigger than, other historic injustices like the infected blood scandal and wrongful prosecution of hundreds of sub-postmasters. Some 20,000 servicemen were forced to witness the testing of British nuclear weapons in the Pacific and Australian desert during the 1950s and 60s Both revealed the human cost of what happens if institutions focus on defending themselves rather than those who have been harmed – and the stonewalling of the nuclear veterans has gone on for seven decades. We shouldn’t be surprised. In the Cold War, they were seen as dispensable human guinea-pigs, needed to help Britain understand how its weapons worked and how its Armed Forces might function on a nuclear battlefield. Pilots flew through mushroom clouds. Servicemen sailed through contaminated waters and crawled across areas exposed to radioactive fallout. Others witnessed nuclear explosions from distances that today seem almost unimaginably close. They were following orders. Serving their country. Doing what was asked of them. Many are no longer with us and those who remain are mostly in their 80s and 90s, still bewildered and angry about why it has taken so long for them to be heard. How shameful that seems when you consider the stories of survivors like John Morris, 88, from Rochdale. He was 18 when he was sent to Christmas Island in the Pacific, where he worked in the military laundry handling contaminated clothing. He vividly remembers being 20 miles from an explosion 20 times more powerful than the bomb dropped on Hiroshima. Although he had his hands over his eyes and sunglasses on, with his back to the blast, the light generated by the blast was so strong that he could see every sinew and bone in his hand. John subsequently suffered from stomach and prostate cancer, and a bone marrow deficiency. He and his wife also lost their baby son Steven from what he believes was the result of radiation. Equally shocking was the experience of Ken McGinley, who witnessed five detonations on Christmas Island as a 19-year-old sapper with the Royal Engineers. He was medically discharged at 21, with a duodenal ulcer which led to two-thirds of his stomach being removed. He also became infertile and developed a rare but treatable type of blood cancer, and before his death in 2024 was diagnosed with stage four lung and kidney cancer. In 1983, Ken had formed the British Nuclear Test Veterans Association to press the Government into action. Other nuclear nations, such as the US, France, even the Soviet Union, acknowledged that the tests happened and created compensation schemes for nuclear veterans who subsequently developed serious illnesses. But British veterans have faced the extraordinary burden of proving their ill-health decades later was linked to their service without access to vital medical records. How can anyone prove what happened when the evidence they need is withheld? In 2007, about 1,000 veterans and their families took the MoD to court in a failed bid to get compensation. Instead of taking the opportunity to right the wrongs of the past, the MoD fought them, in part by instructing the Atomic Weapons Establishment to assemble documentation into a searchable database called Merlin. Astonishing In 2024, the MoD was finally shamed into conducting an investigation that revealed medical examinations and blood testing were carried out for most of that period (stock image) More than 28,000 documents were eventually collected, covering radiation, safety precautions, medical monitoring and what was known about the possible effects of the tests. Yet the veterans’ lawyers were denied access to this archive. In 2022, as chairman of the House of Commons’ Defence Committee, I became aware of newspaper reports that Merlin contained documents referring to blood and urine testing and raised the matter with the MoD. It finally admitted that the Atomic Weapons Establishment held copies of blood-test results and urine-radioactivity measurements and, of course, the astonishing extent of their records was finally revealed following that investigation in 2024. On learning about this, I thought back to the misleading parliamentary answer I had given in 2018. If all this information existed, why had I been told otherwise? Who had colluded to cover it up? I welcome the Prime Minister’s reported commitment to a Hillsborough-style inquiry. But establishing the truth is not the same as delivering justice. The Government should not use another lengthy investigation to postpone recompense indefinitely. There is already enough evidence to justify the creation of a dedicated compensation scheme for veterans and bereaved families, rather than forcing elderly claimants through another exhausting legal battle. Failure This should take account of the health consequences of being deceived for so long. Had veterans and their doctors been given fuller information decades ago, might some illnesses have been identified earlier and suffering prevented? Nor should a formal apology be conditional on every document being recovered. We know these men served in extraordinary circumstances. We know medical monitoring took place. We know important records are missing and that alone warrants a frank acknowledgement of institutional failure. As for me, I would like to make my own apology to the nuclear veterans. I am sorry I didn’t do more to help them sooner. I certainly would have done so had the facts not been withheld from me and not just because I want justice for them. There is also a wider issue at stake here. As the global order fractures and threats to Britain grow, how can we confidently expect a new generation to put on the uniform and perhaps even make the ultimate sacrifice to defend our country if this is how we treat those who served before them? In helping Britain become a nuclear power, John Morris, Ken McGinley and legions of others like them did not hesitate in answering their country’s call. Now it is time for their country to answer theirs.
