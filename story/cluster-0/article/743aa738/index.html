UK travellers warned of disruptions but US FAA allows flights to resume

Article text

The US Federal Aviation Administration has ended its stop on all departing flights in the country “due ⁠to extraordinary circumstances and safety reasons” after the agency earlier reported serious air traffic control communications issues. The announcement means flights are set to slowly resume after hundreds of services were disrupted. The FAA initially halted traffic in Chicago ​due ⁠to air traffic ‌control communications issues on Friday. The agency said: “All departing traffic should remain groundstopped until further ‌notice.” International ‌flights were set to be managed on a case-by-case basis. US Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy confirmed that the stop was due to a communication issue affecting all of the country’s air traffic control centres. He later told a press conference FAA technicians concluded the US’ “incredibly old air traffic control system” had been “overloaded with data”, causing issues with radar and communications equipment. The world’s busiest airspace has ‌experienced setbacks in recent years that have disrupted flights and ‌risked safety. In January 2025, a midair collision at Ronald Reagan Washington National Airport killed 67 people.  Passengers wait at Ronald Reagan Washington National Airport in Washington, DC (Photo: Brendan SMIALOWSKI / AFP via Getty Images) The flight tracking website Flightradar24 had 3,100 flights en route to US airports when the disruption began. It was not immediately clear how the communication issue would affect inbound flights, but by 10.30pm on Friday British Airways had confirmed the cancellation of at least one service due to depart for New York. Responding to concerned passengers on social media, the airline apologised for potential delays to its flights. It added flights were still expected to take off, but “can’t guarantee that this won’t change” even as it advised those expecting to depart to travel to the airport as usual. A spokesperson for Manchester Airport also confirmed three US-bound flights operated by Virgin Atlantic, all scheduled to depart on Saturday morning, would likely be affected if the issue was not resolved quickly. Virgin Atlantic is advising passengers to check for updates at at virginatlantic.com. Meanwhile, Ireland’s Department of Transport advised all travellers departing for the US to check with their airline for updates. “We understand that some flights currently en route to the US are being permitted to land. The US authorities are handling international flights on a case by case basis,” it said in a statement. “This is an evolving situation and the Department advises all passengers who are due to travel to the US on Saturday 14 November to check with their airline for information regarding the status of their flight.” The FAA has indicated normal service is set to resume slowly, meaning many passengers in the US and overseas could still face delays over the coming hours. A passenger looks at a departures board showing delays and cancellations at LaGuardia airport in New York (Photo: Adam Gray/Getty Images) Speaking on Friday, Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin repeatedly told reporters that the situation “isn’t another 9/11”. He said that President Donald Trump was briefed on the situation and ‌all flights were ​accounted for. This is only the second time the US has grounded its departing flights since the September 11 attacks in 2001. A ground stop was issued for less than two hours in January 2023 when a pilot notification system went down. Last year, Congress ​approved $12.5 billion to fix the ‌FAA’s aging communications system, ⁠and on Friday the FAA ⁠and White House asked Congress for another $30.1 billion to ‌address ​other issues. Addressing media after the issue was resolved, Duffy, the Transportation Secretary, said the amount needed to overhaul aging traffic control systems was closer to $50 billion. “What went down was old. It was old legacy equipment that failed,” he added. Speaking at the same press conference, Mullin warned another, similar failure was possible unless Congress was willing to back a “complete overhaul” of the transport system. The outage came amid heightened concern ​about aviation security worldwide after a wave of hoax bomb threats this month disrupted dozens ‌of passenger flights across Europe, forcing unscheduled landings ⁠and prompting fighter jets to escort or intercept aircraft. There was no immediate indication the ⁠US communications failure was related to those incidents or caused by a cyberattack. The US National Weather Service also ⁠said on Friday that it was experiencing ⁠nationwide ​internet ⁠and communications problems ⁠that could ​prevent ⁠users from ‌accessing products on its ‌websites or ‌cause outdated information ⁠to be displayed. There was no immediate indication the ‌two incidents were connected.
