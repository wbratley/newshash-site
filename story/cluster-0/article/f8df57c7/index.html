Why Britain's renewables obsession is failing to bring down energy bills

Article text

We were promised "homegrown, clean energy abundance". We would be off the "fossil fuel rollercoaster", harnessing free wind and sunshine and bringing down bills for good. So consumers may have been shocked to discover this morning that their energy costs are set to rise by £443 from the New Year. The average household bill, under the Ofgem price cap, will rise to £2,166 a year, a 26 per cent increase. During the 2024 election campaign, Labour predicted household energy bills would fall by £300 a year by the end of the decade. Instead, shadow Energy Secretary Andrew Bowie points out they have risen by hundreds of pounds since Labour took office. So what exactly is going on? In 2025, more than half our electricity came from renewable sources, rising to 56 per cent between April and June this year. Surely this 'free' power should see bills going down, not up? Unfortunately, things are not so straightforward. We have already spent tens of billions on a renewables system, and this power comes with hidden costs of its own. While wind and sunshine may be free, the enormous infrastructure needed to turn them into reliable power is anything but. It's true that the latest increase has been driven by the wholesale gas price, a key element of bills. Ofgem estimates that current gas prices are almost two and a half times those seen before the Middle East conflict. Put simply, Britain still relies heavily on gas for heating and electricity generation, so when fuel prices increase, bills go up. This is the 'fossil fuel' rollercoaster the Department for Energy Security and Net Zero frequently refers to. But why aren't our renewables countering this? The price of sunshine hasn't increased, nor has the cost of wind. If more than half of our power is renewable, why aren't our bills coming down? One reason is the "marginal pricing" system used by the UK and Europe. Electricity is sold in a market where the cheapest sources, such as wind and solar, meet demand first. But the price per unit is set by the final, most expensive power station needed to keep the lights on - usually gas. Gas still set the wholesale electricity price around 60 per cent of the time last year in Britain. That means some older renewable generators can receive a higher electricity price, driven by gas, despite burning none themselves. Ministers have chosen not to scrap marginal pricing, which trade body Energy UK says is the most efficient way of ensuring demand is met. Instead, they are expanding fixed-price contracts for new renewable schemes, aiming to reduce the exposure to gas-driven electricity prices. Older generators are also being encouraged to switch to these contracts. The Government hopes to increase the amount of renewable power so that gas sets the price less and less. But removing gas from the system is easier said than done. This is because not all electricity is equal. Renewables can provide huge amounts of energy when the wind is blowing, and the sun shines. But when the outlook changes, the output falls. And we still expect the lights to turn on when we flick the switch. Reliable, 24/7 power requires a backup system of dispatchable energy, and for the UK, that means gas. Nuclear makes up just 12 per cent of the mix, and we closed our last coal-fired power station in 2024. The price of wind and sunshine is not the same thing as the cost of reliable electricity, and UK power is some of the most expensive in the developed world. Additional costs on energy bills, outside the wholesale price, account for some of this expense. These include a range of 'policy costs' such as subsidies to green generators. Households also pay network charges to maintain and expand the grid. Previously, power was dispatched from a small number of coal or gas stations, usually located near demand. But the new system involves an ever-increasing array of new generators, often in remote locations. An upgraded grid is needed simply to handle the increased renewable power. These costs appear on your bill, and Ofgem estimates that some £70billion will be required between now and 2031. As Oxford economist Sir Dieter Helm has pointed out, under the old system Britain had 60GW of electricity capacity to hit a peak demand of 45GW. Because of the intermittency of renewables, we now need double the capacity – 120 GW – to meet a slightly lower peak demand. All of this adds to the bottom line. And the slower the grid buildout, the greater the cost. Already this year Britain has spent £2billion on constraint payments, where remote wind turbines are paid to switch off and gas plants to fire up because the current grid cannot safely dispatch the energy. Grid operator Neso estimates this could rise to more than £7billion a year by 2030. This is on top of the £90billion already paid by consumers in green subsidies between 2002 and 2024, according to figures from the Renewable Energy Foundation. The optimistic Government picture is that a larger grid and a fleet of efficient renewable generators will knock gas further out of the mix, finally bringing the promised savings. Ministers say that the grid had been neglected for decades. Without renewables, they argue, bills would have risen further. Analysis from grid operator Neso supports the prospect of reduced exposure to gas shocks.
