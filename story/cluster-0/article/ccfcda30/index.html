'Complete system reset' needed to get 440,000 young people into work, says major review

Article text

'Complete system reset' needed to get 440,000 young people into work, says major review - Published Sweeping reforms are needed to help 440,000 more young people into work, according to former minister Alan Milburn. Almost one million young people aged 16 to 24 in the UK are not in education, employment or training - or "Neet" - a current rate of 13%, which Milburn wants to fall to around 5% within a decade. His government-commissioned review, due to be published in full on Tuesday, calls for ministers to intervene from early childhood through to adulthood, funded in part by radical changes to benefits for young adults. But Helen Whately, Conservative shadow work and pensions secretary, said Milburn's proposals were in parts "too cautious" and too reliant on the "expensive hand of the state". Many of the review's proposed changes are specific to England, with some welfare and tax measures applying across all home nations. Milburn, a former Labour health secretary, says extensive change is needed to avoid a "lost generation", and address what he describes as a "moral" crisis. The review says that, in 2024/25, for every £1 spent helping young people into work or education, £25 was spent on youth benefits. By 2036, it says the aim should be for that ratio to be £2 on benefits for every £1 on work support. In order to make change of this scale over the next decade, the review will call for: - A new target of 90% of children to be school-ready with basic life skills by five years old (this figure is now 68% in England, with a current government target of reaching 75% by 2028) - More emphasis on work skills in the school curriculum - Instant access to mental health support without diagnosis - A doubling of the number of young apprentices - Incentives for employers, such as lowering the National Insurance rate for young workers At the heart of the changes recommended by Milburn would be a legal entitlement to support, provided through a new "Youth Opportunity Service" in England. The service would put a young person in touch with a named adviser to help to secure them an apprenticeship, college place, supported employment or a job within four months. To pay for these reforms, young adults would no longer be eligible for the health top-up of Universal Credit, which provides extra money for people whose health conditions limit their capacity to work, or Personal Independence Payment (Pip), which helps with the extra living costs of having a long-term health condition or disability. The review's key welfare measures, some of which have already been announced, suggest replacing these benefits for under-25s with a new Youth Allowance at the current base rate of Universal Credit for young people, and a new Additional Cost Allowance to replace Pip. The minority of young people who are unable to ever work would be supported through a new Severe and Complex Needs Insurance. Kat, 21, from Devon, currently gets Universal Credit with the health top-up, so is potentially at risk of receiving a much lower level of benefits under the proposed new system. She has fluctuating physical and mental health problems, including occasional seizures caused by functional neurological disorder, which recently left her needing crutches. Kat says she "really wants to work", but says she has to rely on welfare support as she has been unable to find a job where she can be employed in a "safe way" because of her health. Milburn, a Labour MP from 1992 to 2010, says many young people are desperate for opportunity, but that many get knocked back repeatedly. Josh, 19, has spent three years looking for work while on Universal Credit with the support of youth employment charity Spear. Although they help him with applications to prospective employers, Josh says "none of them are getting back, and if they do they say you've not got the spot". Grace Coates, who manages Spear St Helens, says she wants to see "more openness to employing people with limited experience". Milburn's review will suggest gradually lowering the rate of National Insurance which employers pay for young adult employees to make hiring and keeping them more attractive. The existing Youth Jobs Grant, where employers receive £3,000 for hiring a young person who has been looking for work for six months, would be retained under the recommendations. So would the existing Jobs Guarantee, in which the government underwrites the cost of a paid six-month job. The government has recently announced that young adults will be eligible for the guarantee after 12 months of being on Universal Credit and looking for work, rather than the current 18 months. The Institute for Fiscal Studies has said reforms will have a greater chance of success if support for young people is balanced with incentives for businesses to hire them. It said that while cutting benefit entitlements would reduce the cash young people receive immediately, it might take longer for an increase in employment to make up for that for some young adults. Responding to the Milburn review proposals, the Conservatives' Whately said: "Where Milburn gets it right, we'll back him," but added that the review "fails to bring down the welfare bill, proposes billions more in government spending and won't make the slightest difference for young people stuck on welfare now". Liberal Democrat deputy leader Daisy Cooper said cutting national insurance for young workers would "only partly undo the damage of Labour's job tax". She said her party's plans would "shut off the Neets pipeline at source, with 100 hours of real work experience before 18 and a careers coach for every young person". Reform UK's shadow education secretary Suella Braverman said the 10-year wait to see changes from Milburn's proposals "would be consigning another generation to the scrap heap". "Reform UK would act now to axe Labour's jobs tax, double the number of apprenticeships and end university scam courses that laden students with debt," she said. For the Greens, deputy leader Mothin Ali said Milburn's proposals announced so far were "extremely concerning", adding: "There is an alternative to threatening vulnerable people with benefits they need, an alternative to unsuitable short-term work."
