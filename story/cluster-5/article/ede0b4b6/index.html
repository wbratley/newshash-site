Andy Burnham open to calls for formal apology for Britain’s role in slave trade

Article text

Andy Burnham has gone further than any previous prime minister towards offering a formal apology for Britain’s role in the transatlantic slave trade, in a Guardian article ahead of a Commonwealth leaders’ summit where the subject is expected to dominate. As he prepares to fly to the Caribbean next week, the prime minister said he was open to calls for an official apology on behalf of the state and offered formal talks to those affected, including individual countries and campaign groups at home and abroad. Burnham wrote: “The Britain I believe in is confident, decent and brave. We led the world in abolishing slavery – and we can show leadership again today.” While any formal statement will depend on that dialogue, Burnham gave what amounted to a personal apology, saying he wanted to share his “sorrow and regret” for the pain and suffering of millions of people, and to recognise the way the slave trade still shapes the world now. However, he rejected demands from Caribbean countries and campaigners for the UK to pay financial reparations for its role in the slave trade, maintaining the position of successive governments that British people today could not be held liable for the past. He wrote that reparations were not the “right answer”, adding: “I do not think that British people today can be held liable for historical wrongs, however heinous, which happened in a different age under different laws and in which they played no personal part.” Instead, Burnham said he would launch a new drive to restructure debt for the poorest Commonwealth countries – led by former prime minister Gordon Brown and expected to eventually include organisations such as the IMF – helping them repay debt over a longer period and with better interest rates. Debt servicing costs for the poorest and most vulnerable Commonwealth countries are at their highest in more than 25 years, he added, with more than 40% of those spending more on interest repayments than on health. Burnham’s intervention follows years of pressure from Caribbean states, backed by some African countries, for European nations including the UK, France and Spain to offer reparations – including monetary compensation, a full formal apology, support for health and education and debt cancellation. As recently as last week, the Commonwealth secretary general, Shirley Ayorkor Botchwey, said a formal apology for slavery from King Charles, who will attend the heads of government summit, “wouldn’t hurt” and the “time has come” for a serious conversation on reparations. Writing for the Guardian, Burnham said: “The transatlantic slave trade was a brutal and barbaric violation of those people. They were denied their humanity. They were separated from their families, treated as property and worked to their deaths. “I want to express my sorrow and regret for the pain and suffering of so many millions and to recognise the way it still shapes our world today.” He added: “We are offering a structured dialogue with those affected by the transatlantic slave trade. Listening to calls for a formal apology on behalf of the British state will form an important part of this process and I am open to them.” Burnham’s intervention received a cautious welcome from leading figures in the Caribbean. Dorbrene O’Marde, the chair of the Antigua and Barbuda reparations support commission, said his organisation welcomed the statement as “a movement in the correct direction in the interest of reparatory justice and humanity generally”. But, he added: “We reject the decision that financial reparations will not be on the table.” Ralph Gonsalves, former prime minister of St Vincent and the Grenadines and a senior figure in the Repair campaign, told the Guardian that an apology was the first point in the Caribbean community’s 10-point reparations plan but “though necessary, is insufficient”. He added: “We are pleased to hear that [the UK] comes into a conversation. But it can’t be a conversation about nothing. It has to be a conversation about something. If they are not going to entertain reparations, what is the conversation about? Nothing.” Sir Hilary Beckles, the chair of the Caricom reparations commission, said he believed Burnham “is carrying the broad middle of British opinion on this matter”. He said the call for financial reparations need not be a stumbling block to progress: “There are much broader issues that can occupy that dialogue. So you don’t know what you will emerge out until you have a conversation of decency. “I think the prime minister will find that Caribbean leaders are very reasonable people on the matter of developing a new Anglo-Caribbean bond … words are important, but the spirit of collaboration is more important.” Caribbean leaders have been reacting to Bunham’s announcement. In a statement, the prime minister of Barbados, Mia Mottley thanked Burnham for “breaking new ground” but said she and the UK government “are not yet on the same page on every element”. “On reparations in particular, our views differ, and we will say so plainly and respectfully. Honest disagreement on some questions should not hold back progress on the many others where we can readily agree. “An apology, a commitment to eradicate a mindset that is anchored in Afrophobia and against all things “black”, investment in health and education, development partnership that fights systemic poverty and institutionalised inequality, and fairer terms on debt for vulnerable states, as called for by the Bridgetown Initiative, are a few of the areas where we believe understanding can be reached and settled,” her statement said. “I thank prime minister Burnham and his foreign secretary, Ed Miliband, for breaking new ground and recognising that this is the right thing, and the moral thing, to do by ensuring that we move to the next stage of engagement on this difficult and emotional issue,” it added. Antigua and Barbuda’s prime minister Gaston Browne, who is hosting this year’s CHOGM, welcomed particularly Burnham’s offer to assist with debt restructuring and a transition to renewable enegry. “The Caribbean’s longstanding position on reparatory justice remains unchanged. Differences over financial compensation should be addressed through dialogue, but they need not prevent progress in areas where agreement is possible,” he said in a statement. Jamaica’s prime minister, Andrew Holness, also welcomed Burnham’s willingness to discuss a formal apology. In September, Jamaican officials lodged a landmark petition to the king seeking legal advice on its claim for slavery reparations, which will be heard by the privy council in March. “We have always been open to having full and meaningful dialogue on this important issue. We welcome this development, coming in the wake of our legal petition which is now before the privy council,” Holness said in a statement. Similarly, the Bahamas prime minister, Philip Davis, commended Burnham’s decision to agree to a dialogue and consider an apology. “The Bahamas approaches this dialogue with a clear understanding that the issue is not simply one of historical remembrance. It concerns the enduring consequences of slavery and the importance of justice, acknowledgment and meaningful redress for the generations of Africans and their descendants who continue to live with its legacy,” he said. The UK government has taken a cautious approach to the issue amid pressure from leftwing MPs to offer a formal apology and engage in reparatory justice, alongside concerns over the potential fiscal and electoral impact, with polls suggesting most voters are opposed to financial compensation. The Commonwealth heads of government meeting (Chogm), which King Charles will also attend, runs from Sunday 1 November until Wednesday 4 November. At the previous Chogm summit in Samoa two years ago, Britain’s former colonies agreed “the time has come” for a dialogue about reparations – even though then-prime minister Keir Starmer said it would not be on the agenda. Some inside the UK government had questioned whether Burnham should even attend after the reparations row dominated the 2024 gathering. But his decision to address the issue head-on is likely to be welcomed in Antigua and Barbuda. Campaigners see the summit as the culmination of decades of advocacy from Caribbean leaders for a deeper acknowledgment of the UK’s historic responsibility for the enslavement of millions of Africans. Advocates say the UK had been under increasing pressure to act after a UN resolution in March which described the trafficking and racialised chattel enslavement of Africans as the gravest crime against humanity. The UK was among the 52 countries that abstained, saying that while it recognised the gravity of the issue, it disagreed with the fundamental proposition of the text which it said risked creating a hierarchy of historical atrocities. In June, Mottley, announced a new manifesto from Caribbean leaders asserting the “moral, ethical and legal case” for reparations over damage caused by hundreds of years of enslavement. The 10-point plan argued that repair could include international debt cancellation and that climate justice and slavery reparations were “inextricably linked”. Burnham also referred to the history of Manchester as “the cotton capital of the world” which grew in part thanks to cotton picked by enslaved people. In 2023 the owner of the Guardian apologised for the role the newspaper’s founders had in the transatlantic slave trade, and launched the Cotton Capital series and a 10-year programme of restorative justice.
