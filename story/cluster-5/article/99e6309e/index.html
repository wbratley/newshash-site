Nearly 100 Labour MPs including ministerial aides oppose Rosebank oil field

Article text

Nearly 100 Labour MPs including ministerial aides oppose Rosebank oil field - Published Ministerial aides are among nearly 100 Labour MPs who have signed a letter to the prime minister to express their opposition to the Rosebank oil field. The government could also face resignations from junior ranks if the project goes ahead, the BBC has been told. Energy Secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh is due to make a decision on whether to give the go-ahead for drilling in the oil field off the north-west coast of Shetland within weeks. The government has remained tight-lipped - both publicly and privately - about what it is likely to do. Those close to the energy secretary insist that she has not yet made her decision. But 92 Labour MPs have signed a letter, sent to the prime minister today, urging him not to allow the project to go ahead. Ministerial aides - the most junior rank of government - are among the signatures. The MP who organised the letter, Catherine Fookes, said: "If Rosebank is approved, champagne corks will pop in the offices of oil companies - but it will not make an iota of difference to my constituents' energy bills. "The oil belongs to private companies, and will only be shipped overseas to the highest bidder. "What it will do, however, is accelerate the climate crisis, which is already pushing up food bills, causing devastating winter floods like those we saw in Monmouthshire last year, and fuelling wildfires across our green landscapes in summer." It's the most visible demonstration yet of the deep divides in the Labour Party over the issue. The decision about whether to approve Rosebank involves a legal process which technically lies solely with the energy secretary. Fahnbulleh has, in the past, been regarded as someone personally opposed to the oil field. Others in the cabinet, including Chancellor John Healey, have let it be known that they would like to see the project given the go-ahead; while the prime minister has repeatedly said he wants to take a "pragmatic" approach to North Sea oil and gas. But Fahnbulleh insisted last month that she alone would make the final decision and claimed the courts would ultimately side with her if colleagues attempted to overrule her. Influential pro-Rosebank voices suggested that senior ministers had toned down vocal support as the decision draws closer. They suggested this wasn't a sign that Rosebank would be vetoed, but because any obvious attempts to pressure the energy secretary might mean her final decision would be subject to a legal challenge. A number of MPs say they have heard conflicting claims - with both outcomes being predicted. What is clearer is that the government's decision will be controversial, whichever way it goes. There is a private and public battle playing out. Backers of the scheme argue approving it would provide an economic boost. The Conservatives also say it would enhance energy security. But oil is traded internationally – and the energy secretary has said approving the scheme will not help with immediate cost of living pressures. Rosebank would also be more polluting than Jackdaw, a gas field, which was approved last month. A fortnight ago, backers of the field were confident it would be approved. They believed the chancellor's language on energy bills at the Budget, coupled with the lack of political jeopardy that materialised from the approval of the Jackdaw gas field, had made their case stronger. The same group of MPs believe it would be difficult to reject Rosebank around the same time the prime minister is set to deliver a 10-year plan focused on reindustrialisation. The government has said oil and gas would be part of the energy mix for many years to come and sources in the trade union movement have suggested that if Rosebank doesn't go ahead, existing inward investment in the North Sea would be put at risk. While Labour's manifesto upon entering power in 2024 pledged not to "issue new licences to explore new fields", it does pledge to honour existing ones. In 2023, the then Conservative UK government granted a licence for Rosebank and a legal challenge followed. The Court of Session in Edinburgh ruled last year that the government had made the approval unlawfully, leaving the current Labour government to make a new decision on whether to give the go-ahead. The mood among some pro-Rosebank MPs changed this week, with several who back approval telling the BBC they were now worried it would be blocked. The BBC has spoken privately to Labour MPs involved in the debate, before the letter was sent to the prime minister, to get a sense of how divisive it is proving. One ministerial aide said: "There are quite a few of us who are considering our positions. I'm in that group. We want to see leadership on climate change." Another aide who said they might quit added: "There a group of us for whom we became Labour MPs because of climate change. It is the issue that motivated us into politics." Other aides – known in Westminster as parliamentary private secretaries – told the BBC they were opposed, but would stay in their jobs if it went ahead. Another said they thought there were increasing signs the prime minister would not be able to carry his party with him if the scheme was given the thumbs up. And another said the energy secretary might have to resign if it goes ahead, given her scepticism about the project. It has been reported in recent weeks that members of her team are encouraging opponents to make their feelings known. The BBC has been told the same. One MP, who backs Rosebank, said the behind-the-scenes operation had been so effective, the scheme was up in the air again. That has led to some anger at the energy secretary. One minister says: "Miatta has boxed herself in with how she's handled Rosebank. It's outrageous if she's encouraging MPs to speak up over Rosebank. We should get on and do it." Like the decision itself, the exact timeframe is unclear. But some opposition figures expect it mid-December. Whatever happens, it will be controversial and has already exposed splits in the Labour Party. - Published16 November - Published26 October - Published11 November
