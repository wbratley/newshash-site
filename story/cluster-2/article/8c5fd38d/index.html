Prince Harry says he would re-join the British Army to fight Russia: 'I will 100 per cent be there to serve my country'

Article text

Prince Harry has vowed to rejoin the British Army to fight Russia as he warned that we 'should all be nervous' about the threat of war. The Duke of Sussex, who served for ten years from 2005 to 2015, said that he would '100 per cent be there to serve my country' if the need arose. He made the comments during a visit to Ukraine, where he met soldiers fighting off the Kremlin's invasion. The remarks follow increasing warnings by defence chiefs recently that the UK needs to be more prepared for a war with Russia as Moscow flexes its muscles on its borders with Europe. Harry, 42, said he would be willing to serve in the military again if needed, saying: 'There is a lot that we can learn from Ukraine. I think there's a lot that the whole world can learn from Ukraine. 'If asked to sign up again, if the need is there, then I will 100 per cent be there to serve my country alongside my fellow veterans and service personnel.' Speaking to The Telegraph in the western Ukraine city of Lviv, the prince suggested Britain had been in conflict with Russia for the past decade, even if without 'drones, bullets and rockets'. Harry said he believed Europe had finally 'woken up to the threat' from Moscow, but was worried that the continent could 'turn on itself' rather than concentrating its efforts on thwarting Russian aggression. Prince Harry speaks at a meeting with the Ukrainian Invictus community in Lviv on Saturday Prince Harry races to an Apache helicopter alongside his fellow pilots in Afghanistan during his military service back in January 2013 The Duke of Sussex sits in a helicopter in Afghanistan, when he was serving as a pilot in 2013 with 662 Squad Army Air Corps And the Duke of Sussex also condemned President Donald Trump's approach to Ukraine, which he argued did not reflect the views of the broader US population. Harry served in the British Army for a decade, completing two tours in Afghanistan as an Apache helicopter pilot and rising to the rank of Captain. Since leaving the military, he has been a patron and campaigner for veterans and soldiers, founding the Invictus Games in 2014 to support wounded, injured and sick service personnel and veterans through sports. During his unannounced visit to Ukraine last weekend, he told injured soldiers he would bring Invictus to their country once they had 'won the war'. It marked his fourth visit to Ukraine since the Russian invasion began. On Sunday, he visited the Superhuman Centre – a specialised medical, prosthetics and rehabilitation facility in Lviv. When asked if he was concerned about the current geopolitical situation, Harry said: 'I think we should all be nervous, absolutely.' He added that the UK needed to 'be prepared', and that 'those of us that have served should be ready to serve again'. It comes as Nato leaders are increasingly worried about a possible Russian attack, with cyber-attacks on the rise and spy chiefs suggesting in September that Putin could be preparing an attack in 'months not years'. The British Army currently has a strategic reserve of 95,000 retired regular and reserve personnel who can be called up again to fight in a crisis. Prince Harry also appeared to criticise US President Trump's policy on Ukraine for going against the sentiment of American society. The President had said he was 'glad' Prince Harry and Meghan had left America back in August, when the couple had made the decision to move back to the UK, criticising their treatment of the Royal Family. But the Duke of Sussex said of the November midterm election results, which had seen a huge win for the Democrats, that things were 'going to change' and that is something everyone can be 'looking forward to'. He also noted there was a 'big separation' between what the American people felt in terms of their support for Ukraine and what 'a certain group' – 'or administration' – was doing. While the Prince held the belief that Ukraine was winning the war, he said there needed to be an 'exit strategy for Russia', warning that the UK needed to not become complacent or step back in its support for Ukraine.
