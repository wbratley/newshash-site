Cop31 talks go into overtime as debate rages over lack of fossil fuels in draft deal

Article text

Crucial climate talks headed into overtime on the last official day of the Cop31 UN summit as countries continued to argue over a draft deal that was silent on fossil fuels, with a host of other contentious issues complicating negotiations. Nearly 200 countries struggled to bridge their differences after two weeks of intense talks and impassioned speeches in Antalya, Turkey, about the effects of climate breakdown around the world, with a scheduled finish of Friday evening breached. “At this point, no one is happy,” Brazil’s national secretary for climate change, Aloisio Lopes Pereira de Melo, told reporters. The Guardian understands there will probably not be a new draft of an agreement for negotiators to pore over until Saturday morning. A sprawling initial draft deal released earlier this week included strong calls to accelerate the phase-out of coal, oil and gas, but a second, shorter version on Thursday did not include mention of fossil fuels and weakened goals on electrification. On Friday there was a dramatic intervention by African ministers early on in the day, with one saying: “We didn’t come here to beg.” Civil society groups, who have a mandated role at the talks, declared it the “worst Cop ever”, with none of their priorities making it through the negotiations. They were particularly frustrated by a failure to take forward a just transition mechanism agreed last year, which they hoped would embed social, racial and economic justice into a low-carbon future. Several Cop insiders said Saudi Arabia was one of the countries playing an obstructive role. The petrostate has gained a reputation over the decades for using the Cop process to slow action. Saudi Arabia is understood to be among the countries pushing for carbon capture utilisation and storage (CCUS) to be strongly referenced in the final agreement as a way to help the planet to minimise overshooting the 1.5C temperature goal agreed in Paris. Neil Grant, a senior expert on mitigation at the advisory group Climate Analytics, said CCUS was being put into the text “because the fossil fuel interests want it in there”. He said: “It’s a Trojan horse for fossil fuel interests.” Eight countries called for reference to “a clear transition away from fossil fuels” to be restored into the text of any agreement. Panama, Belgium, Côte d’Ivoire, Finland, Greece, the Solomon Islands and Sweden outlined vital issues they said must be addressed, including ensuring polluting countries paid into funds for developing countries to adapt to the climate crisis. Juan Carlos Navarro, Panama’s climate minister, said: “It is not acceptable to have a Cop or a cover document that does not even mention fossil fuels, and we simply will not put up with it. We need to tell the truth and to speak clearly.” Panama had sent an open letter to the president of the negotiations, Australian climate change minister Chris Bowen, with a list of demands the country said were not negotiable. “Well, red lines – that’s how negotiations work,” said Bowen. On Friday afternoon, he said: “There is still a spirit of making it work. People have strong views, of course. I’m not stepping away from that. But no one has said to me, let’s give up and go home.” In a dramatic press conference on Friday morning, African ministers from Ghana, Zambia, Senegal, Kenya and Sierra Leone said African people were being asked to pay for a crisis they did not cause. They accused rich countries of simultaneously attempting a third exploitation of the continent – after slavery and colonialism – by using carbon taxes to prevent Africans from making use of their wealth of critical minerals to industrialise. Zanetor Agyeman-Rawlings of Ghana, who was ministerial chair of the African group of negotiators, said: “The continent will not accept an outcome that offers declarations without the resources to deliver them. We didn’t come here to beg. We came here to claim what is rightfully ours for our people who are suffering the brunt of anthropogenic activities of developed countries, that they are now being asked to pay for in ways that they never asked.” A vital request of African nations was for discussion of climate finance. All week civil society observers have accused richer countries, led by the EU, of obstructing progress in talks on money. The chief climate negotiator for Europe said on Friday that it was already the world leading climate finance donor. A senior EU official said: “We, together with the EU member states, are providing €32.3bn [£27bn] from public sources and mobilising €9.3bn in private finance in 2025 to support developing countries. We’re doing by far the most.” The last Cop meeting to finish on time was in Italy in 2003. Some talks have dragged into the Sunday after the scheduled Friday finish.
