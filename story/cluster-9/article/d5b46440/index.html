King Harald V of Norway obituary

Article text

King Harald V of Norway, who has died aged 89, Europe’s oldest serving monarch, was the country’s third king since it regained independence in 1905 following the dissolution of its union with Sweden. Although he ceased to be head of the Lutheran state church in 2012 and subsequently lost his constitutional status as “holy” six years later, he did exhibit the Scandinavian moderation, stoicism and genial approachability that led a newspaper to name him Norway’s person of the year in 2017. The first Norwegian king to be born in the country since 1370, Harald was also related to both the Swedish and the British royal families. His parents were Crown Prince Olav, who was king as Olav V between 1957 and 1991, and his wife, Princess Märtha, a member of the Swedish royal dynasty. His grandparents were King Haakon VII, the first king of the independent country, who reigned for 52 years from 1905, and his wife, Queen Maud, daughter of Britain’s Edward VII. The family roots are Danish – from the Glücksburg dynasty – as were those of the Duke of Edinburgh and the former Greek royal family. The mixed heritage made him sympathetic to refugees: “It is not easy to say where we come from. Home is where the heart is,” he said. He was Queen Elizabeth II’s second cousin and a knight of the garter. At the time of his birth, Harald was 16th in line to the British throne, though he had fallen in the rankings to around 80th in more recent years. Confusingly, he was not the fifth Harald to rule his country, but the sixth – the 10th-century Harald Bluetooth generally not being assigned a number in the list of Norwegian kings. The king never attracted a picturesque title as did some of his Viking predecessors – such as Bluetooth, Harald Fairhair and Harald Hardrada, who tried to seize the English throne a few weeks before the Norman invasion in 1066 and was killed at the battle of Stamford Bridge. Harald himself was only three at the time of the Nazi invasion of Norway in 1940, and he and his mother and two older sisters, Ragnhild and Astrid, initially fled to Sweden, which, trying to maintain its neutrality while accommodating the Germans’ attack on their neighbour, was reluctant to let them in. It is alleged that the royal party was initially turned back at the border, and allowed to proceed only when the driver of their car threatened to ram it through the border post. Within three months, Harald, his sisters and mother had escaped to the US. They spent the war years in Washington DC and Harald thereafter spoke English with a slight American accent. Meanwhile, Harald’s grandfather and father escaped to England, where they led a government in exile. Famously Haakon, turning up at the BBC to make a broadcast to his people, was asked by a receptionist: “Where did you say you were king of, dear?” Following the liberation of Norway at the end of the war, the family was reunited and Harald was enrolled first at a state primary and then at Oslo cathedral school. From there he studied at Oslo University and the Norwegian military academy, before following in his father’s footsteps to read history, economics and politics at Balliol College, Oxford, where he also rowed for the college. A keen competitive yachtsman, he and his crews represented Norway at successive Olympics in Tokyo in 1964, Mexico City in 1968 and Munich in 1972, and won world championship medals through the 1980s and a European championship gold medal in 2005. If this was a conventional career, much less so was his determination to marry a commoner, Sonja Haraldsen, a shop assistant who was the daughter of an Oslo textile manufacturer, whom he had met at a sailing party in 1959. The friendship was kept secret for several years, partly because of Olav’s reluctance to have the heir to the throne marry someone who was not royal. In the end, Harald forced the issue by declaring to his father that if he was not allowed to marry her he would not marry anyone, thus bringing an end to the dynasty. The couple married in 1968. Harald was sworn in as king automatically following Olav’s death in January 1991. Although lacking his father’s easy bonhomie, the king attempted successfully – and popularly – to open up the institution and its palace and estates more freely to the public. The Norwegian monarch’s duties are similar to those in Britain, with non-exercised executive power, and all official acts having to be endorsed by the governing council of state, equivalent to a British cabinet. The Norwegian parliament constitutionally debates whether to continue the monarchy every four years, and earlier this year, despite controversies concerning the royal family, voted in favour of its continuation by 141 to 26. The greatest crisis in the country’s modern history occurred on 22 July 2011 when the domestic terrorist Anders Behring Breivik murdered 77 people, mainly at a young socialists’ holiday camp on Utøya island. The king attracted public praise in the wake of the tragedy, when he met bereaved relatives and called for national solidarity and mutual support. He was seen to weep at the memorial service for the victims. Harald was treated for bladder cancer in 2003, contracted Covid, had a pacemaker fitted in 2024 and was being treated for the blood disorder haemolytic anaemia. He is survived by Sonja and their children, Princess Märtha Louise and Prince Haakon, whose wife, Mette-Marit (nee Tjessem-Høiby), was obliged to apologise in February this year for email exchanges with the convicted sex offender Jeffrey Epstein, in which her name was mentioned nearly 1,000 times. The king said that she had not broken any law. Mette-Marit’s son by a previous relationship, Marius, was jailed for four years in June following convictions for two rapes and drug offences. Haakon succeeds Harald as King Haakon VIII and will adopt the monarchy’s traditional motto: “All for Norway”.
