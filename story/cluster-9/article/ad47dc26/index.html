No clear winner in Israeli general election: Exit polls predict Netanyahu's party to win most seats but rival bloc 'will be bigger and could still oust him'

Article text

Benjamin Netanyahu's political future hangs in the balance after polls closed last night in the Israeli election. Early exit polls suggest the 77-year-old's Likud party will remain the largest in the country's parliament. However, Israel faces fresh uncertainty after it was suggested neither of the two main rival blocs had secured enough seats to form a government. The Kan 11 and Channel 13 news channels published polls which suggested that the two rival blocs to Netanyahu would win 52 seats each. Channel 12's polls meanwhile showed the Gadi Eisenkot-led bloc marginally behind on 50. Eisenkot's Yashar party was thought to represent Netanyahu's main opposition. But both camps remain well short of the 61 seats required for a majority, raising the prospect of difficult coalition negotiations and a prolonged political stalemate. Between 2019 and 2022, Israel held five elections and there are fears of a repeat. Arab-led parties, Ra'am and Joint list which also oppose Netanyahu, were earlier projected to secure a record 18 seats, potentially giving them a key role in Israel's political future. A final outcome dependent on the official results is expected in the coming days. Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu is pictured in Jerusalem on Tuesday Netanyahu's main rival, former military chief Gadi Eisenkot, is pictured in Tel Aviv Voters are pictured celebrating the results of early exit polls in Tel Aviv Women were seen popping bottles of champagne while others danced and shouted The election is widely seen as a referendum on Netanyahu's leadership since the Hamas-led October 7 attacks of 2023, as well as the wars which have since shaken the Middle East. A record 74 per cent of Israelis turned out to vote on Tuesday, marking a high since records began in 1974 when citizens voted following the Yom Kippur war against Egypt and Syria. Throughout the day, Netanyahu urged his supporters to cast their votes, calling it an 'emergency' and a 'matter of life and death'. At an election rally later in the day, the country's prime minister told his supporters: 'Likud is the largest party. We must establish a strong, right-wing government.' Likud had earlier requested that voting be extended beyond the country's 10pm [8pm GMT] deadline, but a committee ultimately refused this, saying 'the current circumstances do not justify an extension'. Israelis vote for parties, which then form alliances to create a majority coalition. No single party has ever won a 61-seat majority in the 120-member parliament, or Knesset. Emotions were running high throughout the country on Tuesday with one voter in Jerusalem telling the Daily Mail: 'This is critical, and it's never been as critical.' She said she had come from kibbutz Nirim, which was attacked on October 7. In Kiryat Shmona, a Netanyahu stronghold near the border with Lebanon, a man kicked and tore down a poster of the prime minister's leading rival, former military chief Gadi Eisenkot. In Tel Aviv, some families of those killed in the Hamas-led 2023 attack and families of former hostages held a moment of silence. 'Remember all those whose voices are no more,' said Yona Adam, the mother of Mapal Adam, who was killed. At a polling station set up in a Jerusalem school, some said they were voting for change while others said they only trusted Netanyahu to keep them secure. 'He's not responsible for October 7,' said Nisim Cohen, a reservist. He added that he wants Netanyahu to win the election and continue the wars in Gaza, Lebanon and with Iran until Israelis can sleep peacefully. Zipporah Bennett, an 85-year-old retiree, also said she was voting for Netanyahu 'because he has had the experience of navigating the country through a lot of difficult seasons', praising his support from the US and adding: 'He's a man who's not afraid to take action.' Alexandra, a 65-year-old voter in Jerusalem, said: 'I want change in our country. I want a good democratic centrist government.' Some 7.3 million people are eligible to vote, with hundreds of thousands of young people casting their ballot for the first time. Surveys show they overwhelmingly identify as centre-Right or Right-wing. Tens of thousands of Israelis are estimated to have flown in from abroad to vote in an election seen as crucial to deciding the future character of their country. 'I think this is one of the most important election days in the history of the state of Israel,' Uriel Brandler, a 58-year-old real estate agent, said from Jerusalem the day before the vote, saying he hoped for change. Eisenkot, Netanyahu's top rival, was up and meeting voters as polls opened in Ofakim, one of the communities in southern Israel that was attacked by Hamas-led militants on October 7. Eisenkot lost his son and two nephews to combat in Gaza. The visit was likely aimed at reminding voters of the security failures ahead of the deadliest militant attack on Israel in its history, and of Netanyahu's refusal of a state commission of inquiry into them. Meanwhile, wearing his trademark noose-shaped lapel pin, hard-Right National Security Minister Itamar Ben Gvir cast his ballot in the West Bank settlement of Kiryat Arba, where his supporters had set up banners and speakers Thousands of volunteer Israeli election observers were in place at polling stations across the country to monitor the election. Eyal Sher said he volunteered with a civil society group called Elections Guards because he wanted to play a role in such a critical vote. 'We have a corrupt government that will do anything to stay in power,' he said. 'Every vote counts and we want to make sure the procedures are kept intact.' According to Israel's Central Election Committee, more than 7.3 million Israelis are eligible to vote, including around 330,000 young Israelis who spent their high school years in the shadow of war. Netanyahu has been Israel's most dominant politician for nearly two decades, and a poor showing could end the 77-year-old's lengthy career on a bitter note and open the door to a new era in Israeli politics. A victory would cement his image as the ultimate political survivor. Earlier this month, US President Donald Trump backed Netanyahu's reelection, but the late endorsement still marked a setback for the Likud politician who had billed his tight ties to the Republican president as a selling point for staying in power. Netanyahu has been a master at forming coalitions with religious and nationalist partners. Eisenkot has pledged to establish an independent state commission of inquiry into the security failures that led to the attack, something Netanyahu's government has resisted. Seeking to unseat the premier is an informal bloc of parties led by Eisenkot's Yashar and spanning from the Right to the centre-Left - including former prime ministers Naftali Bennett and Yair Lapid, as well as seasoned politician Avigdor Lieberman and Left-leaning Yair Golan. Neither Eisenkot nor Netanyahu has publicly said they would partner with the predominantly Arab lists, which represent some 21 percent of Israel's population and who could become kingmakers if the results are as close as expected. The deadliest day in Israel's history has featured prominently in the campaign, but there has been almost no debate over Israel's wartime conduct in Gaza, which led to an international arrest warrant against Netanyahu. Israel's occupation of much of southern Lebanon and parts of Syria, as well as its two wars launched against Iran, also has broad support among Israelis. The candidates have instead focused on Netanyahu's role in the security breakdown on October 7 as well as his efforts to exempt ultra-Orthodox Jews from military service and a 2023 judicial overhaul seen by critics as an assault on Israel's democratic institutions.
