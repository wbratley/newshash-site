Why Trump still wants his illegal ‘Liberation Day’ tariffs

Article text

Just a couple of months into his second presidency, Donald Trump sent shockwaves through the world economy with a large board summarising the tariffs he wanted to impose on imports from much of the rest of the world. His executive order declared a national emergency, justifying the tariffs as a response to a vaguely-defined “unusual and extraordinary threat to the national security and economy of the United States”. A year and a half later from what he dubbed “Liberation Day”, the US president is still trying to make the plan a reality. He faces prevailing legal headwinds in a country founded on the principle of “no taxation without representation”. In other words, if you want to slug your citizens with a new tax, you have to get it through Congress. The biggest slap came from the Supreme Court in early 2026. Despite being increasingly stacked with Trump sympathisers, the court ruled 6-3 that Trump’s “national emergency” justification didn’t fly. A furious Trump called the judges’ decision “an embarrassment to their families”. He has since used other methods to try to keep tariffs in place, including a time-limited power to add an import surcharge, followed by another power unlocked by accusing countries of profiting from forced labour. Wednesday saw yet another chapter, with his official trade representative announcing prospective new tariffs on 14 economies including China and the European Union (but not the UK). It comes despite the effect of tariffs on the cost of living in the US being widely accepted as a major factor in the Republicans’ midterm election rout. One study found that tariffs had added about 3 per cent to underlying inflation. Former Democrat congresswoman Kathy Manning called the midterms results “a total rebuke of Donald Trump’s erratic tariff policies”. Yale’s Budget Lab estimated on Wednesday that the new proposal would add another 0.3 per cent to US inflation. Trump made no public comment about the new tariffs. His social media feed was unusually quiet on Wednesday, which he spent at Mar-a-Lago the day before the Thanksgiving holiday. According to the White House press corps torrential rain in Palm Beach ruled out the prospect of playing golf, before an evening meeting with Nato Secretary Mark Rutte. What are the new tariffs US trade representative Jamieson Greer announced on Wednesday he had determined that “the acts, policies, and practices of 14 economies relating to structural excess capacity and production in manufacturing sectors are unreasonable and burden or restrict U.S. commerce, and are thus actionable”. American trade laws give him the power to impose new tariffs if a foreign government is “unjustifiably” restricting US commerce. Greer named 14 economies including China, the EU, India, Japan, Korea and Japan, which he said had “tilted the trade balance in their favour” by supporting “excess capacity and production”. In the case of the EU he singled out the automotive, chemical and pharmaceutical industries. In a 217-page report, he concluded that EU policies were unfairly boosting domestic manufacturing through export subsidies and preferential lending. Greer proposed additional tariffs: up to 20 per cent for China, 25 per cent for India, 18 per cent for other Asian countries and 15 per cent for the EU, Korea, Taiwan and Japan. The plan now goes to public consultation, with hearings early next year. Greer told CNBC said the new tariffs were “targeted to respond to this problem of excess capacity”. “They’re increasing supply overseas without actually increasing consumption overseas,” he said. The new tariffs wouldn’t add to existing trade deals or tariffs, he said: “when it all nets out, it really will just top up a few percentage points”. In the case of China, the tariff rate “will go up some”, he said, but there would be some exempt items under a recent trade agreement. How countries reacted The EU rejected the legal premise of the proposed rules, saying the opposite was the case. “Europe is among those most exposed to overcapacity,” Olof Gill, the Commission’s deputy chief spokesperson, said in a statement. “The EU and the U.S. share a real concern about global overcapacity, much of it state-driven and concentrated in third countries. We regret the lack of joint action.”  Brazil’s government said Washington’s announcement was “unacceptable” and said it “will not yield to such threats”. China said tariff wars were in no one’s interest. “The so-called ‘overcapacity’ does not really exist,” China’s foreign ministry told Reuters. But Greer said China had “five-year plans to build capacity beyond what demand requires”. He also noted that China had a “broad exemption” from the new tariffs on $30bn of products, some of which was agreed at Trump’s recent meeting with China’s Xi Jinping. What the experts say Nick Marro from the Economist Intelligence Unit told the BBC there was a “degree of posturing” ahead of another Trump meeting with Xi Jinping next month in Miami, but the proposed tariff rate was “within the scope” of current agreements, so unless the argument escalates into a tit-for-tat then “US-China ties still look pretty stable”. In the run-up to the midterms, Trump told political rallies his tariff policies were bringing manufacturing back to the US, making the unsupported claim they had created “$25trn in wealth”. But trade policy expert Dmitry Grozoubinski told Al Jazeera “If I wanted to use the US tariff code to encourage people to build factories in the US, I would not do it this way”. “Every time they say the word tariffs, people remember that their costs are really high and get angrier,” he said. “This shows that at least on some level, they are still prepared despite the headwinds, to go ahead and push that button.” Grozoubinski said the excess capacity power was “supposed to be a scalpel, and they used it like a flamethrower. It is not meant to set the US tariff code, which is what this effectively did.” He said the forced labour tariffs were “incredibly dubious”, but the excess production tariffs might be easier to legally defend if those are struck down, to “replace the tariffs that replaced the tariffs that replaced the tariffs that replaced the Liberation Day tariffs”. Stephanie Rickard, professor of political science at the London School of Economics, said the new tariffs on the EU could affect the UK in two ways. “First, EU exporters may look for new markets, and the UK is an obvious option,” she said. “Second, if UK goods face lower US tariffs than EU goods, they will become relatively cheaper in the US, which could help British exporters win new customers there.” Ziran Ding, lecturer in economics at the University of St Andrews, said UK exports may suffer if they are selling parts for products that the EU sells to the US. And if the EU economy suffers, the UK will feel the side-effects. “On the other hand, cheaper goods that are blocked from the US may also flood into the UK,” he said. Jonathan Portes, Professor of Economics and Public Policy at King’s College London, did not expect major knock-on effects for the UK. “Obviously the fact that US policy is erratic, unpredictable and irrational both make things more difficult for the UK and increases the value of closer links with the EU, but we knew that already,” he said.
