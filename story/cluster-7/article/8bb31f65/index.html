Burnham says it would be ‘unthinkable’ to rejoin EU without referendum

Article text

Andy Burnham has said it would be “unthinkable” for the UK to rejoin the EU without holding another referendum, offering his strongest commitment yet to directly consulting the public if he concludes Britain should go back in. The prime minister is planning to set out the long-term options for closer ties with the bloc next month, with concrete steps coming after the next general election, as he has pledged to stick to Labour’s manifesto red lines. Downing Street insiders expect that Burnham would – if he concluded the best route forward for the UK was to rejoin – push for a mandate to start negotiations in the next manifesto, then hold a confirmatory referendum. In a Commons statement on last week’s Brussels summit, Burnham was asked whether he could give a “clear and unequivocal” statement he would not take the UK back in without one. “Yes, it would be unthinkable,” he replied. Burnham has criticised former Tory prime ministers for being “stuck in the past”, dismissing the advice of Rishi Sunak, David Cameron and Theresa May, who have said he should not reignite the Brexit debate. “I’m not sure they’re the people I’ll be listening to here because they left Britain with a bad Brexit deal. They’re stuck in the past because they can’t face up to the bad deal that they did,” he told broadcasters. “I’m not a prime minister that’s going to be stuck in the past. I want to lead Britain forward to a more hopeful future, get the economy growing again, get cost of living down for people, lower energy bills. “All of these things can be achieved by a more positive, constructive relationship with the European Union. I believe it will be better for Britain to take this path, and I won’t be listening to those voices who put us here in the first place.” However, Burnham later told MPs that he would pursue a “pragmatic approach” to the UK’s future relationship with the EU and there was “no fixed opinion” and “no fixed destination” inside No 10, despite publicly revealing that his personal preference would be to rejoin. “I do think it’s important for me to say again that the world has changed and will continue to change, and we need to test any options against that reality,” he said. “But always, we should be, in my view, pragmatic and choose whatever option secures the best deal for Britain.” There are some concerns inside government that with four big EU states, including France, heading to the polls in the next year, the rise of rightwing populist parties could change Downing Street’s calculations. “As we look to the next year, the politics of Europe could change considerably. I think we need to go into the further consideration of options with all of those factors in mind. We’ll have to obviously watch how life changes,” Burnham said. The prime minister told MPs the deal he signed in Brussels on Friday – covering food and drink checks, emissions trading and youth mobility – was a “landmark moment” that would lower the cost of living. The government wants to persuade the public that closer ties with the bloc will boost the UK economy, although some insiders acknowledge the difficulty of making that argument when the global economic outlook remains bleak. Burnham, who will lay legislation next week to implement the food and drink deal, said big supermarkets had confirmed it would “lift the pressure off the weekly shop”. EU officials have indicated that last week’s deal was contingent on the UK aligning tuition fees so that European students would pay the same amount as domestic students, with a six-month deadline to reach a compromise. There is expected to be further discussions on the UK joining the EU’s still-to-be-finalised “Made in Europe” scheme, which protects against cheap Chinese imports, as well as the bloc’s electricity market, with other talks on touring artists and illicit migration. While the bill setting out last week’s “reset” deal will be presented to the Commons this week, the result of the subsequent discussions is unlikely to emerge until the next UK-EU summit, expected next summer at the earliest. Officials in both the EU and UK have signalled they would like to see those talks with Brussels run in parallel to the “national debate” that Burnham has said he wishes to have with the UK public about what the closer future relationship might look like. The two sides agreed last week to a “brainstorming” process to look at the options for closer ties, including options of a Swiss-style deal with close sectoral alignment, a single market and customs union deal or a path for Britain to fully rejoin the EU, with an informal summit next year. “We will say more next month when we publish the plan for Britain about how we move forward. But the initial agreement we have reached … is that it will be an informal exchange of ideas, with the results to be presented around the time of the next UK-EU summit,” Burnham said. The legal text of the deal, expected in the coming days, is likely to contain a clause which would penalise the UK if it reneged, in order to pay the costs of reinstating customs infrastructure, which opposition parties have called a “Farage clause”, should the Reform UK leader win the next election and reverse the deal.
