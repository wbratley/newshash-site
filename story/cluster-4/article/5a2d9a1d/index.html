White supremacist convicted of planning bomb attack on asylum seekers in Hull

Article text

A white supremacist has been found guilty of planning a firebomb terror attack on asylum seekers in Hull. Mikka Anderson, 24, stockpiled weapons including napalm, knives and a crossbow for an assault designed to kill as many people as possible, Newcastle crown court heard. The British man, from Hull, tried to recruit an accomplice for an attack on the city’s Royal Hotel, which housed asylum seekers until September this year. However, his plan unravelled when that man went to the police. Anderson had denied preparing an act of terrorism but was found guilty on Friday. The judge, Mrs Justice Lambert, told the defendant he should expect a “very significant” prison term when he is sentenced next year. Anderson first made contact with his intended accomplice through social media, knowing that the man – who cannot be identified by the media – had been involved in anti-migrant disorder in the city. On a secure messaging platform, Anderson told the man he was in a group of “white Christian nationalists” whose “main objective is fighting back against Islam, paedophile elites and Jews”. The two men met in the city centre when Anderson outlined his plan to attack Hull’s Royal Hotel “and to kill as many of the migrants who were housed inside it as possible”, said Adam Birkby, prosecuting. During the trial, the intended accomplice told jurors Anderson explained his rationale for targeting the hotel, saying: “He said it was full of nonces, rapists, people that shouldn’t be in our country.” After the informer told police about the plot, Anderson was arrested. Knives, a crossbow and petrol bombs were found during a search of his home. He had made or attempted to create napalm, gunpowder and fuses, the court heard, and carried out reconnaissance on the hotel, which housed migrants until September this year. The trial heard that Anderson, who grew up in France and Spain before moving to the North York Moors, had previously tried to join the Royal Marines. Anderson had also tried to buy a piece of land in Devon with fellow rightwingers, who planned to use it as a shooting ground and a site to practise tactics. Earlier, the trial heard, he had looked into Islam but turned away from it and had also previously been a Mormon. He then developed an interest in the extreme-right following the Southport riots, finding a sense of “brotherhood” with others after experiencing an isolated childhood, jurors were told.
