UK weather live: 28 areas on flood alert as Storm Boelo set to bring 80mph winds The Met Office has issued 'danger to life' amber weather warnings for Storm Boelo, which arrives just after Storm Austen caused serious damage in the South West Storm Boelo is set to bring 80mph winds and 'widespread travel disruption' to the UK this weekend, with forecasters warning of potential flooding, power cuts and problems on the roads. One amber warning for wind, two yellow warnings for wind and a yellow warning for rain will cover different areas across the UK this weekend. It comes after Storm Austen led to airports being closed, flights cancelled and even Brighton's iconic West Pier partially collapsing. Meanwhile, Prime Minister Andy Burnham warned on Friday that the UK could face “a winter of greater flooding” and said we are living in a world where “unexpected events” are “more and more common”. An amber weather warning is in place across Scotland today, along with the north of England down to Carlisle and the north-east of Northern Ireland. It comes just a day after Storm Austen battered the South West of England, with schools closed and 31,000 homes at one point left without power yesterday. Tom Morgan, a Met Office meteorologist, said: “Yesterday’s Storm Austen affected the far south of the UK and northern France and the Channel Islands, whereas today’s Storm Boelo will be affecting the north of the UK." Which storms get a name? The Met Office says British storms will be named then they have 'the potential to cause disruption or damage which could result in an amber or red warning'. It adds: "This is based on our National Severe Weather Warnings service, which is a combination of both the impact the weather may have, and the likelihood of those impacts occurring. "Storms will usually be named on the basis of the impacts from strong winds, but the impacts of other weather types will also be considered. For example rain, if its impact could lead to flooding as advised by the Environment Agency, SEPA and Natural Resources Wales flood warnings, or snow. Therefore 'storm systems' could be named on the basis of impacts from the wind but also include the impacts of rain and snow." Why are storms named? The Met Office started naming storms in 2015, and the forecasters said it starting doing so to 'provide a consistent message and aids the communication of approaching severe weather through media partners and other government agencies'. It adds: "In this way the public will be better placed to keep themselves, their property and businesses safe." The storm names are compiled by the Met Office, Met Éireann (Ireland's froecaster), and KNMI (the Dutch national weather forecasting service) - but you can submit your own suggestions via this online form.
