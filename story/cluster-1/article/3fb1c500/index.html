Victims of crime failed at every step of justice system, report warns

Article text

Start your Independent Membership today. Join todayAlready a member? Log in Victims of crime are being failed at every step of the justice system, a major new report has warned. A damning survey of more than 6,000 people has revealed victims feel treated as an “afterthought” as they navigate a fragmented justice system, with poor police communication and unprecedented court delays. The Victim Survey 2026, said to be the biggest and most comprehensive of its kind, revealed that almost half of victims were dissatisfied with their communication from the police. Of those, more than eight in ten said they would not report a crime again. Almost half of respondents had their trials delayed due to soaring crown court backlogs, with the trial taking place more than a year after it was originally scheduled for 28 per cent of victims. One rape and sexual assault survivor was told at 5.15pm on Friday evening that her trial was due to begin the following Monday morning. She did not meet the CPS barrister prosecuting her case until two minutes before she was due to give evidence. Another said her life was “put on hold for another year” and her mental health suffered after a trial for her rape and sexual assault was delayed. A male victim of a hate crime said slow and impersonal police responses “gave me the impression they did not care, like I was wasting their time”. The findings come as some 5,000 prisoners are scheduled to be released early in stages over the next year in a bid to cut overcrowding behind bars, causing more anxiety for their victims. Claire Waxman, the Victims’ Commissioner for England and Wales, said victims are being failed by a system that “too often treats them as an afterthought”. She said prime minister Andy Burnham should make justice reforms central to his 10-year plan for Britain. “This report sends a clear message: Victims are still being let down by a criminal justice system that too often treats them as an afterthought,” she said. “Too many are left chasing updates, fighting for support, and navigating a process that compounds, rather than alleviates, the trauma of crime. “Victims should not have to battle the very system that is meant to protect them. If we are serious about restoring confidence in justice, we need urgent reform that puts victims’ needs, rights, and experiences at the heart of every decision. “After more than a decade of decline, the cracks in the system are too deep to paper over.” Only a third of victims said they were kept informed by the Witness Care Unit about post-charge case progression. A fifth said when their offender had been convicted, the conviction and sentence was not clearly explained to them. The survey also found almost a third of victims chose not to report their crime to the police, citing reasons including lack of trust in police, fearing they would not be believed or the trauma and emotional stress of coming forward. However rates of non-reporting were higher for Black and Asian victims, with 38 per cent of Black and 45 per cent of Asian victims choosing not to report, the report found. The survey also found only 14 per cent were aware of their rights through the Victims’ Code and less than a quarter were referred or self-referred to support services. Ms Waxman is calling for an urgent “end-to-end review” of a victim’s journey through the system. “Victims should experience a seamless service, with a consistent point of contact to guide them through the process, access to support when they need it, and confidence that their rights will be delivered,” she added. Chief executive at Victim Support, Katie Kempen, said: “This powerful research is a damning indictment of the criminal justice system’s failings, revealing the widespread and devastating impact on victims and survivors of crime. “Victims should not be paying the price for a system in crisis. They deserve swift access to justice, safe and functioning courts, agencies that consistently deliver their rights, and independent support services backed by sufficient, sustainable and multi-year funding. “If the government is serious about putting victims first, it must take bold, ambitious action to fix the justice system and ensure every victim gets the support they need.” His Majesty’s chief inspector of the Crown Prosecution Service, Anthony Rogers, said: “The overriding message is that the criminal justice system is not a system at all. It is a combination of silos, in which victims need to do most of the heavy lifting to get any resolution or understanding of what is happening.” Victims minister Alex Davies-Jones said the government is doing “everything we can” to fix the “broken” justice system. “When we came into office, we were faced with a growing court backlog, prisons almost full to the brim and victims waiting unacceptably long for justice,” she said. “We have already taken important steps to fix this, investing an unprecedented £550 million in victims’ services over three years and taking action to tackle the crown court backlog to make sure victims get the fast justice they deserve. “We’re also publishing a new Victims’ Code next year to make it easier for victims to understand, set clear expectations for criminal justice agencies and help victims feel supported. “We will always look at what more we can do to support victims, and I thank the Victims’ Commissioner for this valuable report and her dedicated advocacy for everyone who has been affected by crime.”
