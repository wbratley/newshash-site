Poundland collapses, putting more than 10,000 jobs at risk

Article text

Poundland has collapsed into administration, putting more than 10,000 jobs at risk in “devastating news” for staff in the run-up to Christmas. Administrators at AlixPartners have been appointed to the retailer, which has 600 stores in the UK and Ireland, by Poundland’s directors. The chain is now expected to be broken up, with significant job losses and store closures after its owners rejected at least two last-ditch bids for the budget retailer. AlixPartners said a sales process “did not identify a viable solvent solution for the business as a whole”. It said Poundland employed more than 10,000 people, which the Guardian understands is about 2,000 fewer than were in the business just a month ago. It has been suggested that as many as 30,000 jobs in total could be at risk in Poundland’s stores, supply chain and at other affected businesses on high streets around the country. Poundland would continue to trade with all stores and distribution centres remaining open while administrators pursued “all options including sales of the business and its assets, either in whole or part, from administration”, AlixPartners said. Bally Auluk, a national officer for shopworkers’ union Usdaw, said: “This is devastating news for our members, who were only recently praised by management for helping to turn the business around. “There is never a good time for this to happen, but just before Christmas has to be the worst. This decision has been made by the owners, Gordon Brothers, despite failing to engage with Usdaw, which is a disgraceful way to treat our members.” The US finance firm, which bought Poundland for a nominal £1 from the European retail group Pepco last year, has been under pressure to accept an offer that would save jobs and protect thousands of the retailer’s suppliers. Gordon Brothers said on Wednesday afternoon it had “explored every avenue to secure a sale of the whole company” but that Poundland was “still vulnerable to inflationary pressures or any downturn in sales and margin”. It said: “Poundland has faced several years of instability. In July 2025, Gordon Brothers supported the company’s restructuring plan – stepping in when no one else was willing to finance the business.” In notes seen by the Guardian, Poundland’s directors wrote to staff and suppliers on Wednesday expressing “sincere sadness and intense regret” that they had been forced to call in administrators after Gordon Brothers said it would not provide more funds to keep the business operating as there was “no prospect of an acceptable solvent offer”. Poundland’s managing director, Barry Williams, told suppliers: “We know this outcome will be as disappointing to you as it is to us.” About 2,000 suppliers will lose significant business and millions of pounds in payments for stock already supplied. High inflation and heavy competition from budget stores, online specialists and supermarkets have left the 36-year-old retail chain – the UK’s first and biggest nationwide single-price retailer – struggling. Poundland’s board filed a notice of intention to appoint administrators last month – which was later extended – protecting the retailer from the demands of creditors until 26 November while a solution to its problems was sought. Just two bidders interested in the whole company remained in talks right up until this week’s deadline. Martin Property Group, a Northern Ireland family-owned company, which has been working with the existing management for about two months, remained interested. It was joined by a last-ditch bid from Germany’s Zachert Private Equity, a fund founded by a former Bloomberg journalist that specialises in acquiring mid-sized companies. Other bidders for the whole group included the little-known finance group Xyra Group Holdings, and the Irish investment company Innovest, which both fell out of the bidding race over the past week. AlixPartners said: “We would encourage anybody interested in acquiring the business in part or as a whole, as well as UK retail operators with open employment opportunities, to contact the administrators.” Poundland’s board was forced to appoint administrators before Thursday’s deadline as shelves began to empty because the company was running short of funds to restock during its peak Christmas trading period. Gordon Brothers’ UK director, Mark Newton-Jones, held the latest of several meetings with the business secretary, Jonathan Reynolds, on Monday, more than a week after giving evidence to the business select committee. MPs called on the government to protect Poundland and other high street stores from “predatory capitalism” and asset-stripping last week. Andy Burnham, the prime minister, said: “Poundland is a part of the fabric of high streets across the country and the news it has gone into administration just weeks before Christmas will be deeply concerning for workers and their families. We will make every effort to secure a deal that protects jobs and keeps as many shops open as possible.” MPs have accused the Poundland owner of extracting millions of pounds from the retailer when it was on a “knife-edge”. Documents provided to MPs showed the finance firm had taken close to £20m in interest payments and various fees, including those relating to store closures since acquiring Poundland. Liam Byrne, the Labour chair of the business select committee, said he had recalled Newton-Jones to give public evidence. He said the committee was seeking assurances that Gordon Brothers or any related party would not act in any “insolvency capacity in relation to Poundland”. Poundland, which was founded with one store in Burton upon Trent in 1990, has endured a rocky history under a string of different owners as it has faced rising costs and heavy competition from chains such as Savers, Home Bargains and B&M, as well as from the big supermarkets. Poundland co-founder Steve Smith said he was “gutted” about the chain’s administration. It is now expected to be broken up with retailers such as Poundstretcher, B&M and The Range taking parcels of stores but leaving potentially hundreds empty. Gordon Brothers rejected an initial management-backed rescue bid financed by Martin Property Group for the discount chain. The US-based finance company said it could raise more from a breakup of Poundland – selling off stock and parcels of stores to rival retailers – than through the solvent bids. Those bids promised only a small upfront payment for the £30m in shareholder debts that Gordon Brothers took on with its fellow shareholder – Poundland’s former owner Pepco – at the time of buying the chain.
