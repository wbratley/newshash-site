Pope Leo has lung cancer, Vatican confirms: Pontiff is diagnosed after operation

Article text

Pope Leo has been diagnosed with cancer after part of his lung was removed. The Pontiff, 71, went in for a 90-minute surgery on Tuesday morning after a lesion was found on his chest. The Vatican confirmed in the hours that followed that the nodule had been taken out, but tests found cancerous cells. The examination revealed a diagnosis of adenocarcinoma, a common form of lung cancer. Leo was in the operating room for around three hours, a spokesman said. He added that the surgery was successful and that there were no complications. Pope Leo, pictured on Tuesday morning, has been diagnosed with lung cancer, the Vatican has confirmed Pope Leo XIV arrived at Gemelli Hospital in Rome on Tuesday ahead of the surgery to remove a lung nodule After the operation, Pope Leo XIV woke up normally and returned to his room on the 10th floor of the hospital. It requires a week-long hospital stay and the Vatican on Monday cancelled all Leo's audiences through to October 20. Further tests were being carried out to see if there was any sign the cancer had spread further, a Vatican source added. The Pope had walked into Rome's Gemelli hospital through the main entrance earlier in the day and waved to dozens of well-wishers who had gathered to greet him. Dr Luigi Carbone and Professor Stefano Margaritora issued the following statement regarding Tuesday's operation: 'The Holy Father underwent thoracic surgery as scheduled. 'To ensure a radical procedure, the lower lobe of the right lung was removed using a video-assisted technique. The intraoperative histological examination confirmed the diagnosis of lung adenocarcinoma, as suspected by the pre-operative tests. 'The Holy Father was in the operating theatre for approximately three hours; the operation itself lasted about ninety minutes. The operation was successful and there were no complications. 'Following the operation, the Pope woke up normally and, after a brief period of observation, returned to his room on the tenth floor. 'The surgical team was led by Professor Stefano Margaritora, assisted by Professor Elisa Meacci, Dr Maria Teresa Congedo and Dr Leonardo Petracca Caravella. 'The anaesthesia team consisted of Professor Massimo Antonelli, Dr Giovanni Punzo and Dr Giuseppe Bello.' The Pope was welcomed by Health Director Andrea Cambieri as he arrived at the Agostino Gemelli University Hospital on Tuesday Adenocarcinoma is the most common form of non-small cell lung cancer. While the main cause of lung cancer is usually smoking, this type is more common in non-smokers. The lump was first detected during 'routine checks', according to the Vatican. The American Pontiff has offered a rare level of transparency about the first publicly disclosed major health problem of his 17-month papacy. The Pope revealed the discovery of the small, localized nodule during his Sunday noon prayer and announced that he would have it removed. He said then: 'The surgery is already scheduled for the coming days. Thank you for your spiritual support, and I entrust myself to your prayers.' Experts said prior to the operation that even if the nodule turned out to be cancerous, Leo's prognosis is 'excellent' since it will have been removed at an early stage. A churchman who saw Leo on Monday, Tokyo Cardinal Tarcisio Isao Kikuchi Tsutomu, said the pope told him he felt fine and decided to have the surgery immediately so that he wouldn't have to alter his upcoming travel plans to Latin America. But the decision clearly came as a surprise. While Gemelli has a specific papal apartment that past popes have used, complete with a private chapel on the tenth floor, Leo will actually be staying in a nearby room since the papal apartment is currently undergoing renovations, according to the Vatican press office. The European Institute of Oncology (EIO) advises patients not to fly for three weeks after surgery – which would give the Chicago-born pope just the interval needed to make his planned November 6 to November 17 trip to Uruguay, Argentina and Peru, depending on his recovery. The current itinerary for the trip is ambitious and the longest of his papacy to date, calling for Leo to clock just over 31,000km (19,263 miles) on a dozen flights and two helicopter rides, and a significant altitude change in Peru. 'I think the Holy Father is in excellent hands at Gemelli,' Dr Spaggiari, of the EIO said. 'They have everything needed to keep the Holy Father alive for a long time.' A lung nodule is a small abnormal area measuring less than 3cm (1.2 inches) that sometimes appears on a CT scan of the chest or abdomen. Often, the scans are done to investigate trauma or a lung infection, unrelated to a lung cancer screening. A nodule can be benign or malignant, and oftentimes surgical removal is recommended both to establish whether it is cancer and to treat a malignancy while it is still localised. Pope Francis died aged 88 in April last year of a stroke after a long period at the Gemelli hospital for double pneumonia.
